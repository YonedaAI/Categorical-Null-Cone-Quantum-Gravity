Abstract

Gluing two gravitational regions requires a prescription for the fields they share. At a spatial interface the two descriptions must assign consistent charges to the same corner. At a cut in time a transition amplitude pairs preparations on opposite sides of a common Hilbert space. We study the relation between these operations in a finite boundary model. An orthogonal projector selects the admissible intermediate states. Given an identification of two candidate cuts, their amplitudes agree on specified preparations and detections exactly when the difference of the transported projectors has zero matrix elements between the corresponding probe spaces. Complete probes therefore determine the matching projector. A two-sector slab calculation detects a mismatch between sector selection and the proposed evolution while all contractions remain associative. A separate example shows how compatible local probability measures can fail to extend to a global state. These calculations identify data that a categorical account of null-cone amplitudes must supply, including the identification of the physical cut space and the restrictions under which its pairing has finite norm.

1 A cut through a causal region

An observer sends a signal at p and receives its reply at q. In Minkowski space the part of spacetime accessible during this experiment is the causal diamond J+(p) ∩ J−(q). One can cover the diamond by smaller regions, separate a slice into two pieces sharing a corner, or insert a cut between an earlier and a later preparation. Each operation uses some of the same geometric data. They give maps with different sources and targets.

Example 4.8 in Long’s February manuscript equips a category of Minkowski diamonds and inclusions with covers [1]. A cover gives a place to compare restrictions of field configurations. Chandrasekaran works with intersecting null sheets in first-order vacuum gravity. His matching rules involve fields, frames and orientations, and his composition law evaluates an amplitude between fixed external states [2, Secs. 3.6, 4.4]. The question here is what one can check about the relation between these two constructions before computing a continuum path integral.

Consider an intermediate Hilbert space of dimension two. Suppose the first boundary preparation reaches just the first basis state, and the final detector responds to that same state. Accepting only the first intermediate label gives amplitude one. Accepting only the second gives amplitude zero. Both prescriptions are regular finite sums. Either can be used as a factor in a chain of associative matrix products. The discrepancy lies in what is being summed over. The calculation generalizes to preparations and detectors that probe different subspaces. Their ability to distinguish two prescriptions is exactly the operator norm of a compression of the difference, once one allows all normalized vectors in the probe spaces.

A boundary state space and an amplitude for a region are familiar from the general boundary formulation of Oeckl [3]. Donnelly and Freidel construct extended phase spaces for gauge systems with boundary and exhibit the surface symmetries that act when adjoining two regions [4]. Our finite constructions use elementary representation theory and Hilbert space identities. Their purpose is to make a proposed comparison of gravitational theories sensitive to a wrong identification at a cut. They also give a precise meaning to agreement on a restricted family of physically preparable states.

2 Restrictions, embeddings and propagators

Let 𝒟 be a chosen category of regions and admissible embeddings. An object can carry an oriented boundary, labels on its faces and an allowed choice of frame at a corner. An embedding must specify what happens to those decorations. For instance, a corner that is a boundary component in a small diamond can become an interior surface in a larger one. Comparing an observable attached to that corner requires a rule for identifying the measurement in the larger region. A clean test may begin with a class of regular cuts and a fixed boundary sector.

2.1 Classical descent

For a family F of scalar field configurations, write F(D) → F(U) for restriction along U↪D. Suppose U,V form a cover of D and W = U ∩ V belongs to the site. The sheaf condition for this cover says that

F(D)→F(U) ×F(W)F(V ) (1)

is a bijection. A pair in the fiber product has exactly the same restriction to W. The two directions of the bijection express existence and uniqueness of a global field with those restrictions. Intersections of diamonds need not themselves be diamonds. For a diamond basis one compares the data on all common subdiamonds, or uses a covering sieve.

Gauge fields can agree through a choice of gauge transformation on W. Keeping such a transformation as part of the overlap datum permits automorphisms of a local solution. On triple overlaps the identifications must compose coherently. A groupoid then records the descent data and equivalences between choices. Set-valued matching after passing to orbits too early can discard the map that tells the two frames how to meet.

2.2 Observable algebras

A net A assigns a map A(U) → A(D) to an inclusion. A state on the larger algebra restricts by precomposition. This convention is used in locally covariant field theory [5]. If only A(W), A(U) and A(V ) are given, a candidate universal algebra is the pushout of A(U) ← A(W) → A(V ), in a specified category of algebras. Its universal property concerns maps out of the assembled algebra. A prescription for restrictions of states has a different variance. A positive functional is generally not an algebra homomorphism into ℂ, so the universal property alone does not extend an arbitrary pair of states.

Even a trivial interface illustrates a choice one must make. Take A(W) = ℂ and two copies of the algebra of functions on two points as A(U) and A(V ). Each local algebra is generated by an involution s = s∗ with s2 = 1. Their algebraic pushout in unital complex star algebras has generators s,t with s2 = t2 = 1. Both generators are self-adjoint. The pushout has a representation with

s = ( 1 0 0 −1 ),t = ( 0 1 1 0 ).

Thus 𝑠𝑡 = 𝑡𝑠 is an extra relation. Imposing it yields the four-point commutative algebra. The second choice can express independent classical labels. Choosing the bare pushout fails to impose that physical rule. Similarly a global gravitational algebra would have to encode which observables commute and which boundary charges are shared.

2.3 Temporal composition

A propagator between two boundary preparations has type TM : H𝑖𝑛 →H𝑜𝑢𝑡. Opposite orientations at an intermediate boundary allow one region’s output to pair with the next region’s input. In finite dimension, once this identification and its normalization have been fixed, inserting an orthonormal basis gives

⟨b,T2T1a⟩ = ∑ j⟨b,T2ej⟩⟨ej,T1a⟩. (2)

We take the complex inner product linear in the second argument. Both sides of this equation are complex numbers. Neither a global field nor a map from an algebra pushout is its output. A proposed relation between these operations must supply maps that connect the types in question.

3 Fields on a common corner

In the null-cone construction, two null sheets meet at a codimension-two corner. Small spacelike caps regulate their opposite ends. Chandrasekaran describes spatial gluing by matching the induced fields and identifying states under a simultaneous change of frame [2, Secs. 3.6, 4.4]. For preparations of definite areas A+ and A−, cancellation of the charges of the simultaneous uniform boost requires A+ = A−. Equality of these two numbers tests one charge. The prescription also matches the field data in his Eq. (3.62).

Classically, suppose two Hamiltonian boundary actions have moment maps μL,μR, written in the same dual Lie algebra using a common frame. Choose outward orientations for both sides. The charge of a simultaneous transformation has moment map μL + μR. In the regular setting, reduction at zero involves first taking the locus μL + μR = 0 and then dividing by the diagonal boundary group. A smooth symplectic quotient requires the requisite regularity of the level set and action. At a stabilizer the quotient can be singular. The finite models below do not assume that null gravitational data form a smooth global quotient.

Example 3.1 (Relative signs). A boundary frame sign takes values in {+1,−1}. Record the two signs s,t before either frame has been divided out. A single simultaneous transformation sends (s,t) to (−s,−t). The product 𝑠𝑡 is invariant and distinguishes the two diagonal orbits. Each sign alone has one orbit under the individual sign change. The product of the individually reduced sets therefore has one point. It cannot carry the two values of 𝑠𝑡.

There is a linear version of this calculation. Let a finite group G act unitarily on V L,V R. Suppose the model prescribes the diagonal action U(g) = UL(g) ⊗ UR(g) and requires its invariants. The average

PG = 1 |G|∑ g∈GU(g) (3)

is the orthogonal projection onto (V L ⊗ V R)G. Indeed inversion in G gives PG∗ = PG, and each group element occurs |G|times among products 𝑔h, so PG2 = PG. For any k ∈ G, U(k)PG = PG, and PGv = v on the invariant space. The averaging factor fixes the normalization required for repeated imposition of the same constraint.

Take G = ℤ∕2 and V L = V R = ℂe+ ⊕ ℂe−, where the generator has eigenvalues 1,−1. In the tensor basis ++,+−,−+,−− the projector is diag ⁡ (1,0,0,1). Both ++ and −− have cancelling charges, since the sign representation is self-dual. Projecting separately onto V LG and V RG discards −−. Retaining the full tensor product additionally keeps +− and −+. The common cut space in this example has dimension two. The two incorrect procedures give dimensions one and four.

Proposition 3.2 (Multiplicity of a matched sector). For finite-dimensional unitary representations of a finite group, write V L = ⊕ ⁡ ρML,ρ ⊗ V ρ and V R = ⊕ ⁡ σMR,σ ⊗ V σ, with irreducible complex representations V ρ. Then

dim⁡(V L ⊗ V R)G = ∑ ρ dim⁡ML,ρ dim⁡MR,ρ∨.

Proof. The invariant part of V ρ ⊗ V σ identifies with Hom ⁡ G(V ρ∨,V σ). This space has dimension one when the representations are equivalent and vanishes otherwise by Schur’s lemma. The group acts trivially on the multiplicity spaces. Summing their dimensions proves the formula. □

For a non-self-dual charge the right label must therefore be converted to the dual of the left. If one declares the right Hilbert space to have the opposite orientation at the outset, the dual may already be built into its labels. A comparison of two formulas for gluing must use the same convention. A dimension count can miss a wrong pairing when the two candidate representations have equal dimensions.

3.1 A round corner in a vacuum solution

A common area is only one part of the classical matching conditions. They can be evaluated on an actual double cone. In Minkowski space use coordinates (t,r,𝜃,φ), set c = 1, and take vertices at (t,𝐱) = (−R,0),(R,0), with R > 0. The null sheets meet at the round sphere S of radius R in the t = 0 slice. Choose the cut identification Y to be the identity on the angular coordinates. The four-dimensional vacuum curvature vanishes, but the corner has a nonzero expansion along each of its null normals. It is therefore a useful first comparison for the transport of extrinsic data.

Normalize the future null normals by ℓ = ∂t + ∂r and n = (∂t − ∂r)∕2, so that g(ℓ,n) = −1. Define K(ℓ) = 1 2Lℓq and K(n) = 1 2Lnq, pulled back to S. Writing σ𝐴𝐵 for the unit sphere metric gives

q𝐴𝐵 = R2σ 𝐴𝐵, (4) K𝐴𝐵(ℓ) = Rσ 𝐴𝐵, K𝐴𝐵(n) = −1 2Rσ𝐴𝐵. (5)

Indeed differentiating r2σ𝐴𝐵 along the two normal fields gives 2rσ𝐴𝐵 and −rσ𝐴𝐵, respectively. The expansions are 2∕R and −1∕R. The two trace-free parts vanish. In this frame the normal connection one-form ϖA = −nb∇ ⁡Aℓb is zero. The tangential derivative of the radial unit vector is tangent to the sphere, so it does not contribute when contracted with n.

Use a second frame differing by a smooth rapidity γ : S → ℝ. Thus ℓ′ = eγℓ and n′ = e−γn. Their inner product stays fixed. Upon tangential projection the terms in Leγℓg proportional to derivatives of eγ vanish, since each contains a factor of the normal covector. Consequently

q′ = q,K(ℓ′) = eγK(ℓ),K(n′) = e−γK(n),ϖ′ = ϖ + 𝑑𝛾. (6)

The last equality follows by differentiating eγℓ explicitly: the derivative of the exponential gives −nbℓb𝑑𝛾 = 𝑑𝛾. These are the four matching rules of Chandrasekaran, Eq. (3.62), for Y = 1 in this convention [2]. For example choose γ = 𝜀cos⁡𝜃 with constant 𝜀. Then ϖ𝜃′ = −𝜀sin⁡𝜃 even though the induced metric, its determinant and the area 4πR2 are unchanged. A comparison which merely tests equality of the coordinate components ϖ𝜃′ and ϖ𝜃 would incorrectly reject this change of frame. The 𝑑𝛾 term specifies how the observable must be transported. In contrast dϖ′ = 𝑑𝜛 compares the curvature of the normal connection directly.

The converse mistake is possible too. Suppose only the total area is used to declare that two intrinsic corner metrics match. A round sphere and a nonspherical ellipsoid in a flat spatial slice can be rescaled to have the same area. No intrinsic isometry identifies them. For an ellipsoid of revolution parametrized by

(asin⁡𝜃cos⁡φ,asin⁡𝜃sin⁡φ,c0 cos⁡𝜃),a > 0,c0 > 0,a≠c0,

the intrinsic Gaussian curvature is

𝒦(𝜃) = c02 (a2 cos⁡2𝜃 + c02 sin⁡2𝜃)2.

To check this, the first fundamental form has coefficients E = a2 cos⁡2𝜃 + c02 sin⁡2𝜃 and G = a2 sin⁡2𝜃. The magnitudes of the two principal curvatures are ac0∕E3∕2 and c0∕(aE). Their product gives the displayed formula. It has different values at the pole and the equator whenever a≠c0. Uniform rescaling preserves that distinction. The ellipsoid is a local spacelike cut with null normals. We do not assume it is the intersection of two complete light cones with smooth vertices. The example isolates the failure of area to determine an intrinsic metric. The actual matching rule requires an angular identification Y such that q+ = Y ∗q−.

3.2 The surviving relative boost

The charge conjugate to a corner boost also records the orientation choice. At a fixed angular label strip off the unit solid-angle measure and let k± = ϱS,±2∕(8πGN) be the area density charges of the two faces, following Eq. (3.63) of Chandrasekaran. Write ΓR,ΓL for their boost coordinates, and put

k = (k+ + k−)∕2,d = k+ − k−,γ = ΓR −ΓL,α = (ΓR + ΓL)∕2.

The corner contribution to the canonical one-form is

k+δΓR − k−δΓL = k𝛿𝛾 + d𝛿𝛼. (7)

This identity precedes the constraint. Opposite orientations produce the minus sign. The matching of induced metrics sets d = 0. On the constraint surface, tangent variations have 𝛿𝑑 = 0; the pullback of the two-form in this sector is 𝛿𝑘 ∧ 𝛿𝛾. A simultaneous shift (ΓR,ΓL)↦(ΓR + β,ΓL + β) changes α while leaving γ fixed. It is a null direction of this two-form. Dividing out this shift leaves the relative boost and its common conjugate charge, as in Eq. (3.64) [2]. Variations of the angular identification and charges enter the full expression in Eq. (3.66). Equation (7) is the boost sector of that result.

For the constant mode on the round sphere the integrated charge is 𝒦S = A∕(8πGN) = R2∕(2GN). If a comparison sums both outward charges with the same intrinsic sign before imposing the matching condition, it assigns 2𝒦S to a uniform simultaneous boost, where Eq. (7) gives zero charge for the matched gauge shift. Fixing an external frame can make a boost at a genuine exterior boundary observable. An artificial internal seam carries different data. The transformation at this seam is simultaneous on the two copies. This is the feature represented by the diagonal finite group action above.

4 The physical cut projector

Let H be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space of extended boundary data. An orthogonal projection P : H → H selects the physical data for the cut. Write K = im ⁡ P with the induced inner product and ι : K↪H for the isometric inclusion. Then ι∗ι = 1K and ιι∗ = P. For a left preparation kernel A : X → H and a right transition kernel B : H → Y , passage to physical states gives

(𝐵𝜄)(ι∗A) = 𝐵𝑃𝐴. (8)

Choosing an orthonormal basis of K resolves the identity on K. Representing that resolution on H gives P. This places Eq. (2) and the projected contraction in the same convention without adding a constraint twice.

A diagonal special case has H = ℂI for a finite label set I and Pei = piei with pi ∈{0,1}. In the column convention for amplitudes,

(𝐵𝑃𝐴)𝑦𝑥 = ∑ i∈IB𝑦𝑖piA𝑖𝑥.

A change of basis in H generally makes P nondiagonal. The amplitude stays the same if A,B,P transform together. Carrying across only the selected numerical labels can change the physical subspace.

Lemma 4.1 (Repeated cuts). For two fixed intermediate projections P1,P2 and three kernels A : X0 → H1, B : H1 → H2, C : H2 → X3, contracting either adjacent pair first gives

(CP2B)P1A = CP2(BP1A).

A cylinder acting as the identity on the physical subspace has extended kernel Pi, and inserting a second copy there leaves the contraction unchanged.

Proof. Matrix multiplication is associative. The second assertion follows from Pi2 = Pi. Equivalently, insert the isometries ιi and their adjoints in Eq. (8). □

A non-unit weight w on one admissible label would give w2 after inserting two copies of the same weighted diagonal matrix. A resolution of the identity written in a nonorthogonal basis can include a nontrivial weight, but the dual basis or inverse Gram matrix also occurs. For example, in K = ℂ let v = 2e with e of norm one. Then |v⟩⟨v| = 4 ⋅ 1K. Dividing by the Gram factor ⟨v,v⟩ = 4 restores the identity. A repeated-cylinder check can expose a normalization error before one compares different cuts.

More generally, let F : ℂn → H map coefficient vectors to preparations, and suppose F has independent columns. The span inherits the Gram matrix G = F∗F. The projector onto the span is FG−1F∗, since this operator fixes the columns of F and vanishes on their orthogonal complement. If the columns are dependent one first divides the coefficient space by ker⁡F, or uses the inverse on (ker⁡F)⊥. A finite positive Gram matrix in a model therefore tells one how to remove a redundant label. The operation presupposes a specified pairing of preparations.

4.1 Observables on the matched space

A restriction to physical states has a natural linear map on observables: compress an extended observable a to c(a) = ι∗𝑎𝜄 on K = im ⁡ P. Although this map preserves the adjoint and the unit, it can fail to preserve multiplication. This matters when c is proposed as one leg of the functor to star algebras. For two bounded operators a,b on H,

c(𝑎𝑏) − c(a)c(b) = ι∗a(1 − P)𝑏𝜄. (9)

The extra term is a path through the discarded sector between the two insertions. A measurement on one cut alone can miss it.

Proposition 4.2 (Multiplicative compression). For a unital complex star subalgebra B ⊆ End ⁡ (H), the restriction of c to B is an algebra homomorphism if and only if P commutes with every element of B.

Proof. If P commutes with B, then (1 − P)𝑏𝜄 = 0 for all b ∈B, so Eq. (9) gives multiplicativity. Conversely take a = b∗. The right side is the positive operator ((1 − P)𝑏𝜄)∗((1 − P)𝑏𝜄). If the compression is multiplicative, this positive operator vanishes, hence (1 − P)𝑏𝜄 = 0. Thus K is invariant under every b. Applying the same argument to b∗ makes K⊥ invariant under b. Consequently 𝑃𝑏 = 𝑏𝑃. □

For a worked boundary algebra take the two sign representations on each side of the seam above. Write Z = diag ⁡ (1,−1) and let X interchange the two signs. The diagonal gauge transformation on the extended space is Gd = Z ⊗ Z. Its invariant-vector projector is P = (1 + Gd)∕2. The full algebra of invariant operators consists of all matrices commuting with Gd, which are exactly the two diagonal blocks for its +1 and −1 eigenspaces. Each block is two-dimensional as a state space. Restriction to the +1 block gives a surjective star homomorphism from M2(ℂ) ⊕ M2(ℂ) onto M2(ℂ). The other block is its kernel. The comparison distinguishes an invariant extended observable from its action on the selected physical sector.

Consider the insertions a = X ⊗ 1 and b = 1 ⊗ X. Each changes parity and thus has zero compression to K. Their product 𝑎𝑏 = X ⊗ X preserves parity and interchanges e+ ⊗ e+ with e−⊗ e− on K. Hence c(𝑎𝑏) is a nonzero involution even though c(a)c(b) = 0. Working with the full invariant extended algebra permits the joint observable directly. Building a joint measurement only from the two independently compressed charged insertions would make the signal vanish. In a gauge theory a pair of charged insertions may require a specified connector to define a gauge-invariant observable. Here the common diagonal action states explicitly how the finite labels are identified between the two factors.

The lost signal can be read in normalized states. Let

ψ± = e+ ⊗ e+ ±e−⊗ e− 2 .

Every product of observables invariant under the separate sign changes is diagonal in the tensor basis and has the same expectation on ψ+ and ψ−. In particular the area-like label probabilities are each 1∕2. By contrast the joint invariant X ⊗ X has expectations +1 and −1, respectively, since it interchanges the two terms in each vector. Tracing over one factor on either state gives the same maximally mixed single-factor density matrix. Thus local probabilities can agree and still leave a measurable relative phase at the seam.

This model gives a concrete order for comparing observables on a glued region. Specify the shared gauge action, form its invariant observables, restrict to the physical states, and check multiplication on the proposed domain. If two competing prescriptions each claim to give a physical matrix algebra, the expectations on ψ± test whether they have both retained the joint invariant. In the gravitational problem the area constraint is accompanied by the local matching of the metric, normal curvatures and connection in Eq. (3.62). Which gravitational observable plays the role of the joint insertion is a question about those data. The sign model provides the algebraic test without identifying the gravitational answer in advance.

5 Which changes of cut can be detected

Compare cuts with extended Hilbert spaces H,H′, orthogonal projections P,Q, and a proposed unitary identification U : H → H′. This identification is an input. In gravity constructing it from moved boundary frames would itself be a result. For vectors ℓ,r ∈ H reached by the left preparation and time-reversed right preparation, define the two amplitudes

a(ℓ,r) = ⟨r,𝑃ℓ⟩,a′(ℓ,r) = ⟨𝑈𝑟,𝑄𝑈ℓ⟩. (10)

The use of U on both sides means that the external experiments have already been identified. If a change of cut changes either adjacent kernel in another way, the identification of those kernels requires its own test.

Let L and R be the spans of the available preparations and detections in H. For a fixed pair of exterior maps one may take L = im ⁡ A and R = im ⁡ B∗. Write ΠL,ΠR for orthogonal projections onto these spaces. When several maps are available, take the span of their images, provided any left experiment can be paired with any right experiment under comparison. Amplitudes are linear in the incoming vector and conjugate-linear in the outgoing vector. Equality on all such pairs thus implies equality on the two spans.

Theorem 5.1 (Comparison on accessible boundary data). In the setting of Eq. (10), put D = P − U∗𝑄𝑈. The following conditions are equivalent.

  1. a(ℓ,r) = a′(ℓ,r) for all ℓ ∈ L,r ∈ R.
  2. D(L) ⊆ R⊥.
  3. ΠRDΠL = 0.

If L,R are nonzero, the maximal absolute discrepancy for all unit vectors in the two spaces is

max ⁡ ℓ∈L,r∈R ∥ℓ∥=∥r∥=1 |a(ℓ,r)−a′(ℓ,r)| = ∥Π RDΠL∥. (11)

In particular complete preparations and detections give the same amplitudes if and only if 𝑈𝑃 = 𝑄𝑈.

Proof. Subtract the second amplitude from the first to obtain ⟨r,𝐷ℓ⟩. This vanishes for every r ∈ R exactly when 𝐷ℓ belongs to R⊥. Applying that statement to each vector in L gives the first two equivalences. For ℓ ∈ L,r ∈ R the same difference is ⟨r,ΠRDΠLℓ⟩. Cauchy-Schwarz bounds its modulus by the norm on the right of Eq. (11). If the compression is zero, both sides vanish. Otherwise choose a unit vector in L attaining the norm, which exists in finite dimension, and let r be its normalized image. For L = R = H the compression equals D. The equation D = 0 is equivalent to 𝑈𝑃 = 𝑄𝑈 by unitarity. □

With one fixed pair A,B, the tested condition is 𝐵𝐷𝐴 = 0. Measuring only one exterior amplitude could leave a discrepancy undetected. Equation (11) refers to coherently superposable and normalized boundary vectors. If the apparatus can realize only a few vectors in those spaces, that equation gives an upper bound on the discrepancy of available experiments. Recovering a complex amplitude from transition probabilities also requires phase-sensitive preparations or interference measurements.

Example 5.2 (Equal dimension at two cuts). Set H = H′ = ℂ2, U = 1, P = |e0⟩⟨e0| and Q = |e1⟩⟨e1| for orthonormal e0,e1. Choose the exterior maps A : ℂ → ℂ2,𝐴𝑧 = ze0 and B = A∗. Then 𝐵𝑃𝐴 = 1 and 𝐵𝑄𝐴 = 0. The trace and rank of P equal those of Q. Every fixed-cut contraction obeys the preceding lemma. If a physical frame change interchanges e0 and e1 and the external maps are also transported, the swap unitary intertwines P,Q and restores equality of the two amplitudes. The measured discrepancy therefore depends on which identification the boundary experiment fixes.

The same projectors become indistinguishable for incomplete probes. Let L = R be the line spanned by (e0 + e1)∕2. Both amplitudes on its normalized generator equal 1∕2. In this case D maps the probe line to its orthogonal complement. Agreement in this restricted experiment says exactly what the theorem permits even though the selected intermediate sectors are orthogonal.

A continuous perturbation of the identification gives a quantitative test. Keeping P = |e0⟩⟨e0|, set v𝜃 = cos⁡𝜃e0 + sin⁡𝜃e1 with 0 ≤ 𝜃 ≤ π∕2 and Q𝜃 = |v𝜃⟩⟨v𝜃|. For U = 1, the self-adjoint matrix P − Q𝜃 has trace zero and determinant −sin⁡2𝜃. Its norm is sin⁡𝜃. The single probe e0 sees the smaller difference sin⁡2𝜃. A measurement confined to an eigenstate of the original sector can therefore underestimate a small coherent error at first order in the rotation angle.

5.1 Moving a cut through a slab

A geometric change of cut can change both of the pieces being sewn. Assume the two spacelike cuts bound a slab with unitary evolution U : H0 → H1 on the chosen extended model. Let A : X → H0 denote the region before that slab and B : H1 → Y the region after it. A measurement at the exterior uses a ∈ X,b ∈ Y . Assign the slab to the later piece, select the physical sector P on the earlier cut, and the total map is 𝐵𝑈𝑃𝐴. Assign the same slab to the earlier piece, select Q at the later cut, and the total map is 𝐵𝑄𝑈𝐴. The exterior kernels of these two decompositions are thus (A,𝐵𝑈) and (𝑈𝐴,B). Their difference factors exactly as

𝐵𝑈𝑃𝐴 − 𝐵𝑄𝑈𝐴 = B(𝑈𝑃 − 𝑄𝑈)A. (12)

Writing ℓ = 𝐴𝑎 and r = U∗B∗b reduces its matrix element to the difference in Eq. (10). Here the transport is dynamical. A coordinate change of one and the same surface uses a frame identification in place of the slab evolution. The algebraic test has the same form only after the two maps on either side have been identified by the relevant rule.

For an open experiment the operator B(𝑈𝑃 − 𝑄𝑈)A measures a possible inconsistency between a transfer and a sector selection. When 𝑈𝑃 = 𝑄𝑈, either placement of the admissible cut gives the same map. Choosing a candidate U that does not satisfy this relation may be exposed by exterior states. It does not produce two incompatible physical predictions of a theory that already supplies an intertwining propagator. The two-sector example in particular tests a proposed identification. It gives no evidence for a failure of gravitational diffeomorphism invariance.

Example 5.3 (A two-reading transfer). In a fixed extended basis let

U𝜃 = ( cos⁡𝜃 −sin⁡𝜃 sin⁡ 𝜃 cos⁡ 𝜃 ),P = |e0⟩⟨e0|.

Use 𝐴𝑧 = ze0 and 𝐵𝑣 = ⟨e1,v⟩. The earlier placement gives BU𝜃𝑃𝐴 = sin⁡𝜃. Using the transported sector Q = U𝜃PU𝜃∗ at the later cut also gives sin⁡𝜃. At 𝜃 = 0 even that detector distinguishes nothing. This is a calculation in a finite model with a declared slab transfer. It displays how both adjacent kernels change when moving a cut.

When the exterior states are fixed, there is a comparable test for a slab described by a general bounded operator S : H0 → H1. Equation (12) still holds with S in place of U. Put L = im ⁡ A ⊆ H0 and R1 = im ⁡ B∗ ⊆ H1. Equality of the two total maps holds exactly when ΠR1(𝑆𝑃 − 𝑄𝑆)ΠL = 0. Its proof uses the same orthogonality argument and requires neither an inverse nor common dimensions of the two cut spaces. When S is an isometry, pulling both detections back by S∗ need not suffice to recover every discrepancy. Components of 𝑄𝑆ℓ orthogonal to im ⁡ S can still be detected at the target. The isometric refinement proposition concerns a comparison using only the transported preparations and detections. If its hypotheses yield the full intertwining relation, this possible leakage vanishes as well.

5.2 A local transport equation

Given a one-parameter family of cuts, a differential test identifies where an inconsistency appears. Work in a fixed finite-dimensional trivialization of the extended cut spaces. Let H(t) = H(t)∗ be a continuous family of Hamiltonians and let the slab propagator U(t,0) satisfy 𝑖ℏ∂tU = H(t)U, U(0,0) = 1. Let P(t) be a differentiable family of orthogonal matching projectors. If the external preparations move by U, complete agreement for all t means P(t) = U(t,0)P(0)U(t,0)∗. No differentiability of the actual gravitational clock projection is assumed here.

Proposition 5.4 (Transport along a family of cuts). Under these hypotheses the propagated sectors agree at every time if and only if

Ṗ(t) + i ℏ[H(t),P(t)] = 0 (13)

at every time. For an approximate assignment, with t ≥ 0,

∥P(0) − U(t,0)∗P(t)U(t,0)∥≤∫ 0t ∥Ṗ(s) + i ℏ[H(s),P(s)]∥𝑑𝑠.

Proof. The derivative of the transported-back projector is

d 𝑑𝑡(U∗𝑃𝑈) = U∗ (Ṗ + i ℏ[H,P])U.

It vanishes exactly when the expression in parentheses does. The initial value is P(0), which proves the equivalence. Integrate the derivative in the finite-dimensional normed space of operators and apply the triangle inequality and unitary invariance of the norm. □

Differentiating P2 = P gives 𝑃Ṗ𝑃 = (1 − P)Ṗ(1 − P) = 0. The commutator in Eq. (13) has the same vanishing diagonal blocks. A first-order defect transfers vectors between the selected range and its orthogonal complement. Measurements confined to the range at the initial time can therefore miss it to first order. The rotated rank-one example exhibits the corresponding quadratic dependence when both probes are restricted to that range.

In a finite model a smooth clock C(t) whose spectrum maintains a gap about a window boundary has a smooth spectral projection there. A circle in the complex plane enclosing the chosen eigenvalues expresses it as a contour integral of the resolvent of C(t). If an eigenvalue crosses the boundary, the rank of the selected projection can jump and no such fixed contour exists over the crossing. Unitary transport preserves rank. A comparison of sharp clock sectors through that event therefore requires either a transported window, a smaller interval of cuts, or a rule that changes the boundary experiment. Smooth energy filters of the kind used in Appendix E.1 have different behavior at a crossing, since the corresponding eigenvalue weight can vary continuously.

The differential test depends on a connection or a trivialization. Under a time-dependent unitary V (t), the new propagator from zero to t is V (t)U(t,0)V (0)∗. Substitution in the evolution equation gives

H′ = V HV ∗ + 𝑖ℏV ˙V ∗,P′ = V PV ∗.

The extra term cancels the derivatives of the moving basis in Ṗ′ + i[H′,P′]∕ℏ. Conjugating the derivative of U∗𝑃𝑈 also shows that the norm bound in Proposition 5.4 is frame invariant. Applying Eq. (13) to a sequence of unrelated coordinate bases without this term would test the motion of the coordinates as well as the proposed dynamics.

5.3 Adding boundary labels under refinement

An embedding of a coarse space in a larger one is often more natural than a unitary isomorphism. With U∗U = 1H, the expression U∗𝑄𝑈 is a positive contraction. It need not be a projection. An exact comparison on all coarse probes imposes a further condition on its range.

Proposition 5.5 (Isometric refinement). Let U : H → H′ be an isometry and P,Q orthogonal projections on H,H′. Then U∗𝑄𝑈 = P if and only if 𝑄𝑈 = 𝑈𝑃. When this holds, im ⁡ U reduces Q, and Q can still have any orthogonal projection on (im ⁡ U)⊥.

Proof. Suppose U∗𝑄𝑈 = P. For x ∈ im ⁡ P,

∥(1 − Q)𝑈𝑥∥2 = ∥x∥2 −⟨x,𝑃𝑥⟩ = 0.

For x ∈ker⁡P, ∥𝑄𝑈𝑥∥2 = ⟨x,𝑃𝑥⟩ = 0. Decomposing any x into these summands gives 𝑄𝑈𝑥 = 𝑈𝑃𝑥. Conversely the intertwining equation and U∗U = 1 give U∗𝑄𝑈 = P. Invariance of im ⁡ U follows from intertwining. Self-adjointness makes its orthogonal complement invariant as well. A projector there has no matrix elements against im ⁡ U. □

For example, embed ℂ on the first coordinate of ℂ2. If the refined sector is the diagonal line through (1,1), compression of its projector to the coarse line gives the scalar 1∕2. That scalar fails the repeated-cylinder check for a one-state cut. Conversely, adding a second orthogonal sector to a previously matching sector changes the dimension while leaving all original matrix elements intact. Refinement agreement on the coarse probes consequently cannot by itself determine the entropy of new fine sectors.

5.4 A sufficient gluing rule for a diagram

Take a category whose objects are decorated cuts and whose generating arrows are region pieces. Work first in the free path category on this graph. Choose a projector Ps at every object s and a linear operator Te : PsHs → PtHt for each generator e : s → t. On a path define the map by composition. This gives a functor from the free category because concatenation composes operators and the empty path acts as the identity.

Suppose the intended geometric category identifies paths through specified pairs of decompositions of the same region. The assigned operators descend to the quotient category exactly when they agree on every generating relation. For necessity, equal arrows must have equal images. For sufficiency, composing an equality of operators on either side preserves it, as does transitivity. All path equivalences generated by the relations therefore have equal images. Theorem 5.1 checks the special case where two decompositions have transported exterior kernels and differ only by a matching projector. It says nothing about decompositions that change the exterior kernels arbitrarily. Constructing a presentation which generates all physical changes of cut remains a geometric problem.

This elementary observation fixes the role of associativity in the conjectured functor Z. Associativity makes a path of declared pieces unambiguous. The additional relations declare when two paths represent a single cobordism. In particular, the identity for disjoint closed amplitudes is a scalar relation. Extending it to a symmetric monoidal functor on boundaries requires specified Hilbert space identifications under disjoint union, coherence for their permutations and compatibility with the region maps.

6 Positive pairings and the sum over states

Appendix E.1 of Chandrasekaran’s manuscript gives five conditions on Lorentzian preparations. The first concerns diffeomorphisms preserving boundary conditions and independence of intermediate cuts, including allowed pairings of matching labelled boundaries. The second imposes conjugation under orientation reversal and positivity of the Gram forms obtained by gluing to reflected preparations. The third gives multiplication of closed amplitudes under disjoint union and normalizes the empty configuration. The fourth gives continuous unitary time translations. The last uses smooth compactly supported energy filters on preparations, finiteness for compatible closures, and a positive spectral measure with a lower bound on its support [2, App. E.1]. The finite projections used here do not model all five conditions. In particular the physical time evolution, closed trace and energy generator are independent inputs.

Suppose a complex vector space of formal preparations has a positive semidefinite sesquilinear form b, computed by gluing and reflection. Cauchy-Schwarz implies b(n,w) = 0 for all w whenever b(n,n) = 0. The null vectors form a linear subspace N, so b induces an inner product on the quotient. If T is to act on that quotient, it must satisfy 𝑇𝑁 ⊆ N′ at the target cut. Boundedness in the induced norm then yields a map of Hilbert completions.

For a concrete example give ℂ2 the pairing b(z,w) = z0¯w0. Its radical is ℂe1. The matrix interchanging e0 and e1 preserves the ordinary Euclidean norm of coefficient vectors, but it sends the null preparation e1 to one of physical norm one. Two representatives of a single physical state can then have different images. Checking positivity on a cut and checking that the next region acts on the cut space are distinct operations.

For temporal sewing of two fixed pieces, Eq. (4.29) in Chandrasekaran resolves the identity at a spacelike cap with matched data. Its Eq. (4.30) bounds the absolute sum by the norms of the two exterior states, using unitarity [2, Sec. 4.4]. In ordinary Hilbert space language if x = 𝐴𝑎,y = B∗b ∈ H, then for an orthonormal basis {ej},

∑ j|⟨y,ej⟩⟨ej,x⟩|≤∥y∥∥x∥. (14)

The bound applies when P = 1 on the physical Hilbert space. For an extended space use ι∗𝐴𝑎,ι∗B∗b ∈ K. Bounded A,B suffice here. An operator-valued distribution at a point or a generalized eigenstate requires a prescription to produce finite-norm vectors first.

6.1 Clock windows and their transport

The projector in the comparison theorem has a counterpart in the source with a specific physical meaning. In Appendix E.3, Chandrasekaran denotes by PD the clock projector for a causal diamond and writes the transformation of both the averaged clock ū^∗ and PD under the unitary V induced by a boundary identification [2, Eq. (E.16)]. A change of variables by the angular map Y identifies the null generators before this conjugation. This example fixes which part of the cut data a categorical comparison must calculate. The uniform boost charge in the preceding section and the clock are different observables.

For clarity, suppose a clock C on H is self-adjoint. Its spectral measure EC gives a projection P = EC(I) for an allowed Borel window I. Relabel the cut by a unitary V and keep the numerical window fixed in the identified clock convention. Then

C′ = V CV ∗,E C′(I) = V EC(I)V ∗. (15)

For a finite spectrum this follows by mapping each eigenspace by V . For a self-adjoint unbounded C the conjugated operator has domain V Dom ⁡ C, and the transported projection-valued measure represents C′ by the spectral theorem. If the convention for reading the clock also changes, its window must transform with it. In particular, if C′ = V CV ∗ + s1, the correct comparison uses I + s. Holding I fixed then selects different preparations near a window endpoint.

To see the effect, consider the smallest clock model with an admitted state and an excluded state. Let c0 < c1 be two possible clock readings and let C = diag ⁡ (c0,c1). Choose an upper endpoint strictly between the readings, so P = |0⟩⟨0|. View a matrix a = |0⟩⟨0| as a preparation. A clock-selected closure in the finite model gives Tr ⁡ (𝑃𝑎𝑃) = 1. Let V exchange the two readings. The transported preparation and projector are a′ = |1⟩⟨1| = P′, so Tr ⁡ (P′a′P′) = 1. If only the preparation is transformed, Tr ⁡ (Pa′P) = 0. The calculation compares the same two outcomes as Example 5.2, now through the closed trace rather than an external vector. The values c0,c1 are labels of the model. Identifying an actual gravitational pair of such eigenstates would require constructing the clock spectrum of the chosen sector.

For general admissible preparations the source’s Eqs. (E.15a,b) read

ζL(cl ⁡ Σa) = Tr ⁡ HΣa^,⟨a,b⟩Σ = Tr ⁡ HΣ(a^∗b^).

The second formula is a reflected pairing. In a d-dimensional clock-selected sector the trace is defined on every matrix, and |Tr ⁡ HΣa|≤ d∥a∥, the estimate in Eq. (E.17). Summing ⟨ej,aej⟩ over an orthonormal basis proves it. In an extended space the trace on 𝑃𝐻 of the compression ι∗𝑎𝜄 is Tr ⁡ H(𝑃𝑎𝑃), which explains the projector placement in the two-reading calculation.

One must distinguish restricting each preparation to an allowed cut from projecting only after a series of unrestricted transitions. With P = |0⟩⟨0|, let a = |1⟩⟨0|. Projecting a preparation at each end gives 𝑃𝑎𝑃 = 0; its reflected norm in that restricted matrix space is zero. The path starting in |0⟩, passing through |1⟩, and returning has Tr ⁡ (Pa∗𝑎𝑃) = 1. An extra projection at the intermediate cut removes that path. Hence a model that allows an insertion which changes the sector must specify which windows apply at its two ends. This is a domain question about the experiment. Eq. (E.16) transports windows already belonging to a declared boundary identification.

6.2 The measure in a filtered closure

A compactly supported energy filter in Appendix E.1 is a function of the generator of boundary translations. It need not be idempotent. In a finite model take distinct energies 𝜖j ≥ 𝜖0, with eigenspace projectors Πj of ranks mj, a Hamiltonian Hb = ∑ ⁡ j𝜖jΠj and T(t) = e−𝑖𝑡Hb∕ℏ. For g ∈ Cc∞(ℝ), Fourier inversion with the sign convention of Eq. (E.4) gives

Fg = ∫ ℝǧ(t)T(t)𝑑𝑡 = ∑ jg(𝜖j)Πj,ǧ(t) = 1 2𝜋ℏ ∫ ℝg(E)e𝑖𝐸𝑡∕ℏ𝑑𝐸. (16)

The rapid decay of ǧ gives convergence in operator norm. Closing the reflected pair as in Eq. (E.5) therefore produces

Tr ⁡ (Fg∗F h) = ∑ jmjg(𝜖j)¯h(𝜖j) = ∫ ℝg(E)¯h(E)𝑑𝜈(E),ν = ∑ jmjδ𝜖j. (17)

Here mj is the multiplicity of an energy in this one matrix representation. A trace on a direct sum of matrix blocks can also carry weights on the blocks. The two sources of multiplicity must be identified before comparing a spectral count.

Take a three-dimensional physical space with Hb diagonal of spectrum 0,E,E for E > 0. A smooth filter equal to one at zero and zero at E closes to 1; a filter zero at zero and one at E closes to 2 in the reflected norm squared of Eq. (17). The single formula Tr ⁡ 1 = 3 leaves ambiguous which window has the larger weight. Swapping the multiplicities of the two energies retains the total dimension and changes those filtered measurements. With all compactly supported filters available, the measurements determine a finite spectral measure. An isolating bump about each eigenvalue determines its mass. When an admissible boundary frame preserves the Hamiltonian by unitary conjugation, transporting its filters and trace preserves this whole family of numbers.

The Euclidean continuation of this finite translation is the positive cylinder Cs = e−sHb∕ℏ for s > 0. Composing two half cylinders and reflecting gives

⟨x,Csx⟩ = ∥Cs∕2x∥2,Tr ⁡ C s = 1 + 2e−𝑠𝐸∕ℏ

in the three-state example. The τ-closure of a cylinder in Appendix E.2 obeys the corresponding positivity under its assumptions [2, Eq. (E.8)]. In the finite model, Tr ⁡ Csn∕(Tr ⁡ Cs)n is the nth trace moment of the normalized matrix Cs∕Tr ⁡ Cs. Thus the same spectral weights affect a replica calculation even when two proposed cut spaces have the same dimension. The energies in this example belong only to its chosen Hamiltonian. A comparison of black-hole entropy would require the actual clock or energy ensemble and gravitational measure derived in the source.

In an infinite-dimensional sector, Euclidean damping and finite norm of individual states must be checked separately. For example on ℓ2(ℕ) take Hbej = 0 for all j. Then Cs = 1 is bounded and satisfies reflection positivity, while Tr ⁡ Cs is infinite. Even a compact filter equal to one at zero has infinite trace. Equation (14) still bounds any one matrix element sewn between two finite-norm vectors. Thus that estimate does not provide the finite spectral weight in Eq. (E.5). In the causal diamond case Chandrasekaran invokes finite dimension for a fixed clock range. In the boundary sector for anti-de Sitter space he instead uses a thermal bound to obtain finite rank on bounded energy intervals [2, App. E.3]. These are different reasons for a closed contraction to exist.

A finite-window estimate gives a version of the latter argument useful for the comparison. Let Π and Θ be finite-rank orthogonal projections of ranks r and s, and write ∥⋅∥2 for the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. For any bounded B,

∥ΠBΘ∥22 = ∑ i≤r ∑ j≤s|⟨fi,Bej⟩|2 ≤ min ⁡ (r,s)∥B∥2

in orthonormal bases of the ranges. The sum over i for fixed j is at most ∥Bej∥2, giving the bound with s. Taking adjoints gives the bound with r. If a net of bounded insertions Bi converges strongly to B, the finitely many indicated matrix elements converge. Hence the compressed difference tends to zero in reflected norm, which is the step used in Eq. (E.18). Without the window a strongly convergent sequence can fail this test: the rank-one projections onto the successive coordinate vectors in ℓ2(ℕ) converge strongly to zero and all have Hilbert-Schmidt norm one.

To remove windows for a fixed Hilbert-Schmidt preparation a, choose increasing finite-rank projections Pn tending strongly to the identity. For a finite-rank sum f = ∑ ⁡ k|uk⟩⟨vk|, ∥PnfPn − f∥2 → 0 term by term. For a general a approximate it in Hilbert-Schmidt norm by such an f. Left and right multiplication by orthogonal projections are contractions in that norm, so ∥PnaPn − a∥2 ≤ 2∥a − f∥2 + ∥PnfPn − f∥2. This recovers the reflected amplitude for this preparation. A moving sequence of states whose support escapes each window is a different question. It is one reason a physical cut comparison must specify both the states being compared and how its regulator moves.

6.3 Two infinite cuts without energy filters

Appendix E restricts the allowed closures by its spectral and finiteness condition. Outside that filtered domain, the estimate in Eq. (14) does not bound the absolute sum over two independent intermediate indices after inserting a resolution at each cut. There is a distinction between composing bounded operators successively and treating all terms of a double series as an absolutely integrable family. An explicit example quantifies the gap.

Example 6.1 (A block Fourier kernel). Let H = ⊕ ⁡ n≥1ℂdn with dn = 4n, and let F = ⊕ ⁡ n≥1Fdn be a block diagonal unitary, each Fd the discrete Fourier transform in its normalized convention. Put x = y ∈ H, with every entry in block n equal to 1∕(ndn). Thus ∥x∥2 = ∑ ⁡ n≥1n−2 < ∞. The scalar ⟨y,𝐹𝑥⟩ is finite. The formal double expansion of this amplitude in the two coordinate bases is

∑ i,jyi¯F𝑖𝑗xj.

Within block n, every one of the dn2 terms has modulus 1∕(n2dn3∕2). Hence the absolute block sum is dn∕n2 = 2n∕n2 and the full absolute sum diverges.

For the same example the normalized Fourier transform sends a constant vector to the first coordinate vector times the square root of the block size. The actual amplitude is therefore ∑ ⁡ n≥11∕(n2dn). Computing 𝐹𝑥 first or F∗y first gives that value. Finite-dimensional cutoffs tending strongly to the identity also recover it as an operator matrix element. Indeed for orthogonal cutoffs Pm,Qn → 1 strongly the difference between ⟨y,QnFPmx⟩ and ⟨y,𝐹𝑥⟩ is bounded by ∥y∥∥F∥∥(Pm − 1)x∥ + ∥(Qn − 1)y∥∥F∥∥x∥. This estimate does not license arbitrary permutations of the individual terms in the double sum. Absolute convergence of a single resolution in Eq. (4.30) remains intact.

One can impose exactly such a finite energy window on the example. Assign energy n to every vector in the nth block. A smooth compactly supported filter then meets only finitely many blocks and the filtered double series is absolutely summable. For a filter equal to one on the first N levels and zero on all higher levels, its absolute sum is ∑ ⁡ n=1N2n∕n2. It has no uniform upper bound as the support grows. Thus fixed-window sewing and removal of all windows are distinct assertions. The example tests extrapolation beyond the single-cut estimate. It supplies no obstruction to the filtered closures for which the preprint gives its trace bounds.

These two limits matter in a gravitational application. Removing an energy filter from amplitudes changes their domain or norm bounds. Removing a regulator from a corner can also change the state space. A proposed comparison has to specify both limiting spaces, the identifications into them and the topology in which the sewn matrix elements converge. Reordering a finite projected sum tests none of these limits.

7 States on an overlapping cover

Even prescribed local probability states with equal overlap restrictions may fail to extend to a positive global state. The example below uses ordinary commutative finite-dimensional algebras. It illustrates why local state compatibility needs its own hypothesis during spatial descent. The sheaf treatment of measurement contexts and marginal distributions by Abramsky and Brandenburger places such extension problems in a more general setting [6].

Consider three binary labels a,b,c ∈{+1,−1} and the three pair contexts 𝑎𝑏,𝑏𝑐,𝑐𝑎. On each pair assign probability 1∕2 to the two unequal outcomes and zero to the two equal outcomes. Each one-label marginal is uniform. Thus all overlap restrictions agree. A putative distribution on the eight global assignments would have to assign probability one to each of a≠b,b≠c,c≠a. Finite additivity and positivity make their intersection an event of probability one. It is empty because the first two inequalities imply a = c. The prescribed local states have no joint probability distribution. The obstruction comes from the cycle of contexts. Moving around its three edges would reverse a binary value three times and return it to its starting label. A loop in a cover of gravitational regions does not itself produce a contradiction. To apply this test one would need actual local boundary observables, their identified overlaps, and a jointly testable extension requirement. No gravitational claim about a particular diamond cover follows from this example. It rules out using overlap agreement as a general proof of state descent.

By contrast, consider only the pair contexts 𝑎𝑏,𝑏𝑐 with joint distributions p𝑎𝑏,p𝑏𝑐 and a common marginal pb. A joint distribution exists:

p(a,b,c) = { p𝑎𝑏(a,b)p𝑏𝑐(b,c)∕pb(b),pb(b) > 0, 0, pb(b) = 0.

Summing over c gives p𝑎𝑏 wherever the marginal is positive, and the entries for a zero marginal vanish by positivity. Summing over a gives p𝑏𝑐 by the same argument. This construction assumes conditional independence of a,c given b. A general joint extension need not have it. For uniform independent pair marginals one can correlate a,c perfectly or leave them independent, with the same pair data. Even on this acyclic cover existence need not imply uniqueness. A set-valued sheaf condition as in Eq. (1) asks for both.

8 A comparison of gravitational preparations

A suitable first experiment has two neighboring vacuum regions with regular null faces meeting a specified corner and spacelike caps carrying initial and final data. Choose a state with specified boundary area range, orientation and smearing. The independent categorical construction should specify what a preparation is, how it pairs with a reflected preparation and which interface observables encode the corner data. Matching local star algebras on the two sides supplies part of this information. For a fixed state it also induces the familiar map between cyclic GNS spaces. It does not specify the preparation made by a different region or the relative phase of two transition kernels.

To see the phase issue, keep the observable algebra and state fixed on every boundary. On a path of regions suppose unitary kernels TM implement prescribed transformations of observables by conjugation. The replacements TM′ = ei𝜃MTM induce the same conjugations. Their composition agrees with TM2∘M1′ exactly when 𝜃M2∘M1 − 𝜃M2 − 𝜃M1 is an integer multiple of 2π. Conditions on phases that can be absorbed into boundary-state choices are a further freedom. An algebra comparison alone cannot fix a relative phase between decompositions of a closed experiment. A system of normalized preparations or a corresponding dynamical datum must be included in a comparison of amplitudes.

Once both theories produce kernels, transport the adjacent preparations into a common cut space and select the same corner data. Compare the normalized matrix elements in the physically available preparation and detection spaces. If those spaces are small, classify the undetected discrepancy by the compression in Theorem 5.1. If either construction changes the external kernels on moving the cut, test their transport before attributing any disagreement to a sector projector. An explicit mismatch could thereby be assigned to a particular operation, such as reversing the orientation of a charge or normalizing the reflected pairing.

For a change of frame with boundary actions V (g) on H and V ′(g) on H′, a physical transport should intertwine these actions on the common data. In a finite diagonal-group model this suffices to intertwine the averaging projectors of Eq. (3) by linearity. A noncompact surface symmetry group may have no normalized Haar measure, and a required invariant may only exist as a distribution. The normalized finite average then has no automatic continuum counterpart. Reconstructing the physical inner product directly from gravitational preparations is one possible way to address that question.

The July representation-theoretic manuscript distinguishes its finite geometric construction from the problem of Lorentzian reconstruction [7]. Likewise the invariant spaces and finite kernels used here already come with boundary labels, a pairing and cut identifications. An intrinsic construction of them from pregeometric data would require, among other steps, an identification of the measurements that determine a frame and of the causal order that distinguishes the two caps. The corner calculation can therefore be carried out on a prescribed geometry before addressing the origin of that geometry.

8.1 Reconstructing the comparison from amplitudes

It is possible to state a finite realization problem in which the identification of cut Hilbert spaces is an output. This reverses the hypothesis of Theorem 5.1, which starts from a proposed transport. One needs labelled, independently defined preparations in the two theories and their complex overlaps, including phases. The labels represent the same exterior boundary experiment in the two constructions.

Theorem 8.1 (Comparison from reflected preparations). Let s range over a collection of cut objects and let Js be a finite set of preparation labels. In theories k = 1,2 suppose that {v𝑠𝑗k : j ∈ Js} spans a finite-dimensional Hilbert space Ksk. Suppose also that for every generator e : s → t a kernel Tek : Ksk → Ktk is given. ⟨v𝑠𝑖1,v 𝑠𝑗1⟩ = ⟨v 𝑠𝑖2,v 𝑠𝑗2⟩, i,j ∈ J s, (18) ⟨v𝑡𝑖1,T e1v 𝑠𝑗1⟩ = ⟨v 𝑡𝑖2,T e2v 𝑠𝑗2⟩, i ∈ J t,j ∈ Js. (19)

Proof. For either theory let Fsk : ℂJs → Ksk map the jth coordinate vector to v𝑠𝑗k. Equation (18) makes the positive Gram matrices (Fsk)∗Fsk equal. Consequently ∥Fs1c∥2 = ∥Fs2c∥2 for all coefficient vectors c. In particular the two maps have the same kernel. The prescription Ws(Fs1c) = Fs2c is well-defined and linear. It preserves the inner product by the Gram equality, has range Ks2 by the spanning hypothesis, and is unique on Ks1 by that same hypothesis. Equation (19) now gives

⟨Wtv𝑡𝑖1,(W tTe1 − T e2W s)v𝑠𝑗1⟩ = 0.

The vectors in the first slot span Kt2, so every difference in the second slot vanishes. Spanning at the source gives the operator identity. Conversely an intertwining family of unitaries with the stated action preserves both sets of matrix elements. □

This construction uses only measured amplitudes in the finite algebraic sense. It does not require one to guess a preferred basis of physical states. A linear dependence between two putative preparations appears as a null vector in the common Gram matrix. This allows coefficient labels to be redundant without assigning extra states to a cut. The construction also shows why measuring a Gram matrix on each object does not suffice: the mixed matrices in Eq. (19) specify how a region acts between the resulting quotient spaces.

A simple test uses three preparations in a two-dimensional cut space: v1 = e0,v2 = e1,v3 = (e0 + e1)∕2. The Gram matrix is

( 1 0 1∕2 0 1 1∕2 1∕2 1∕2 1 ).

It kills the coefficient vector (−1∕2,−1∕2,1). If a second construction declares these three labels to be orthogonal unit vectors, it gives a different reflected amplitude even before evolution. If it gives the same Gram matrix and interchanges the labels e0,e1 on a transfer with an identity transfer in the first theory, Eq. (19) detects the discrepancy through the amplitude from e0 to e1. A matching object dimension and matching counts of preparation labels would miss both tests.

The unitaries in Theorem 8.1 also intertwine every product of the generator kernels. This is a consequence of inserting Wt∗Wt = 1 at each intermediate cut. If the first theory obeys a relation between two paths, the corresponding operator relation holds in the second. A separate argument is needed to say that a pair of geometric paths belongs to the intended relation. Similarly, matching transition kernels on a spanning set does not identify all observable algebras by itself. One must decide which insertions count as observables and add their matrix elements or an equivalent generating condition.

For infinite-dimensional cuts one can repeat the Gram argument on finite linear combinations of preparations. Dense span in each of the two completed cut spaces yields a unitary extension. For bounded transition maps the intertwined equality extends by continuity. For an unbounded field insertion one needs a common invariant core or another precise uniqueness statement for the operators. Equality of matrix elements on a merely dense set can leave room for different self-adjoint extensions. An independently derived finite sector with bounded insertions would avoid that difficulty in an initial comparison.

8.2 Recovering a complex overlap

A reflected preparation often gives a squared norm directly. If the experiment also allows coherent sums of two preparations with controlled relative phase, four such closures determine their complex overlap. Write q(v) = ∥v∥2, with the linearity convention of Eq. (2). For v,w in the same physical cut space, direct expansion gives

4Re ⁡ ⟨v,w⟩ = q(v + w) − q(v − w), (20) 4Im ⁡ ⟨v,w⟩ = q(v − 𝑖𝑤) − q(v + 𝑖𝑤). (21)

The sign in the second equality depends on the convention for the inner product. Taking v = v𝑡𝑖 and w = Tev𝑠𝑗 supplies the mixed matrix element in Eq. (19) from closures on the outgoing cut. These formulas require knowledge of the normalization of the sums. Changing the normalization of a reflected amplitude changes the recovered matrix element, even when the corresponding normalized rays are unchanged.

For example, let v = e0 and w± = (e1 ± ie0)∕2. The probabilities |⟨ej,w±⟩|2 are each 1∕2. But ⟨v,w±⟩ = ±i∕2, and q(v − iw±) − q(v + iw±) = ±22. This experiment uses a common reference phase between the two preparations on the outgoing boundary. It gives a direct readout for a phase difference which was invisible to fixed-basis transition probabilities.

A superselection rule can deny that reference by preventing the superposition of distinct charge sectors. The interference measurement then applies separately within the sectors where coherent additions are allowed. Eq. (E.1c) of Chandrasekaran imposes positivity on specified linear combinations of preparations with matching boundaries in the admissible domain [2]. It does not assert access to arbitrary superpositions of preparations with incompatible boundary sources. The labels used in Theorem 8.1 must therefore specify the sector and the allowed combinations. If only real superpositions are available, the first line of Eq. (21) still measures the real part, while the imaginary part requires additional information.

8.3 Relative phases around a cycle

A preparation labelled only as a ray has a phase ambiguity. If every region kernel is known only up to a scalar phase, even a full set of matrix elements of its induced transformation on observables leaves a cycle measurement undetermined. This ambiguity can be written directly on a diagram. Let Te : Ks → Kt be unitary and Te′ = zeTe with |ze| = 1. Rephasing the entire cut basis at s by ws gives the relation ze = wtws¯ for a phase that can be absorbed in boundary conventions. Along any closed oriented walk the edge phases then multiply to one, using inverses for reverse traversal.

Conversely, on a connected graph with reverse transport available, suppose every such product is one. Choose a base object s0, assign it phase one, and let ws be the product of the edge phases along any path from s0 to s. Two paths followed in opposite directions form a closed walk, so this value is well-defined. Appending one edge yields ze = wtws¯. This gives a finite test for removable boundary phases. It does not assume that all physical phases around loops must be trivial. A measured nontrivial product matters only for paths that the candidate geometric theory declares to represent the same process.

For a square of cuts a,b,c,d compare paths a → b → d and a → c → d. Suppose every physical space is ℂ and all kernels have modulus one. All conjugations on the algebra ℂ agree. The relative phase

z𝑏𝑑z𝑎𝑏z𝑐𝑑z𝑎𝑐¯

is invariant under the four choices of object phases wa,wb,wc,wd. It equals the phase that would appear between coherent alternatives through the two paths. Treating the paths as one and the same geometric decomposition requires the ratio to equal one after all boundary action phases have been accounted for. The compact calculation displays the extra information carried by a normalized gluing amplitude.

9 Conclusion

The choice of a cut fixes which boundary preparations can propagate through a shared interface. In a finite extended Hilbert space that choice is an orthogonal projector. A disagreement between two choices is visible precisely through its matrix elements between accessible preparations and detections. An algebraic comparison of two local theories would gain physical content by deriving those matrix elements from both sides for a decorated diamond with a movable cut. The geometric identification of the boundary data and convergence of the resulting continuum amplitudes are the next inputs required by that experiment.
