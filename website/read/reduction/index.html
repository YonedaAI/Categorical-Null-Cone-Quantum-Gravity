Abstract

A boundary frame can carry a measurable charge even when a simultaneous change of two frames is gauge. We compute the reduced Poisson algebra, the Koszul complex and a quantum normalizer quotient for a family of linear frame constraints. A relative coordinate and its momentum survive both classical and quantum reduction. Repeating a matching condition creates a class in negative degree without changing either ordinary observable algebra. On a graph, a consistent quantum level requires the signed charge offsets to sum to zero around every cycle. A family whose constraint loses rank shows that specializing a reduced algebra can miss observables of the exceptional fiber. We use displacement probes to compare corresponding Gaussian states and exhibit a composition phase invisible in every observable automorphism. For the null-frame boost integral we compute a regulated matching kernel and distinguish its continuum limit from projection onto equal discrete eigenvalues. These calculations give explicit criteria for comparing the area charge, preparation and gauge data attached to a causal diamond.

1 The choice at a corner

Suppose two observers describe the same cut using different normal frames. If each observer is free to change the common reference, only a relative reading can be measured. If one observer changes a frame while the physical field and the reference on the other side stay fixed, the change can act on a charged state. A prescription that calls both transformations gauge has already decided which observables can exist.

Chandrasekaran gives this distinction a geometric expression in his null-cone construction [1]. His equation (3.40) varies a pullback by varying the field and the boundary embedding. The corner term in (3.41) contains the boost parameter and the area form. Equation (3.43) identifies the integrated boost generator with the corner area divided by 8πGN. At fixed area, a boost acts on a state by a phase in the representation of §4.2. A correct dictionary for the algebra must respect this action whenever the boundary conditions retain it. Donnelly and Freidel describe the general distinction between gauge transformations and physical surface symmetries in extended phase space [2].

Long’s site of diamonds specifies where local data can be compared [3, Example 4.8]. His later finite reconstruction starts from relational data on a graph [4]. Neither construction specifies a quantum moment map for changing a null frame. Conversely, a reduced phase space for a particular null region does not specify which categorical probes distinguish two boundary states. The proposed comparison poses a concrete question. Which map of gravitational data carries two equivalent representatives to equivalent representatives and carries a measurable boundary quantity to the same quantity in the enlarged region?

We study that question in polynomial mechanics. A boundary has a coordinate recording a frame and a conjugate momentum recording its charge. The diagonal shift of two frames provides one gauge direction. A third observer can check the matching using the difference of the two readings. All rings used for the explicit calculations have characteristic zero. Each model has finitely many canonical pairs and an infinite-dimensional polynomial algebra. No choice of spacetime, smearing kernel or Newton constant enters these calculations.

2 Descent and admissible observables

Write π : X → X∕∼for the quotient by an equivalence relation. For any set V , a function a : X → V has a unique factorization through π exactly when a(x) = a(x′) for every related pair x ∼ x′. Indeed, the required factor must take [x] to a(x), and the displayed equality is precisely the condition for this formula to have a value independent of the representative. This argument applies to completely general equivalence relations. Differential or operator structure on the quotient needs additional hypotheses.

If i : X → Y is equivariant for two actions of the same group G, the pullback of a G-invariant function on Y is invariant on X. The pullback satisfies

(i∗a)(𝑔𝑥) = a(i(𝑔𝑥)) = a(𝑔𝑖(x)) = a(i(x)).

One can also transport along a group homomorphism by taking i(𝑔𝑥) = ρ(g)i(x) and requiring invariance under its image. The direction of this map matters. Restricting classical fields on a large region to a smaller one can induce pullback of observables from the small region to the large one. Constructing the restriction itself requires compatible boundary conditions. An inclusion of point sets does not define it.

Let R be a commutative Poisson algebra, I an ideal and {I,I}⊂ I. Define

𝒩cl(I) = {a ∈ R : {a,I}⊂ I}.

Then I ⊂𝒩cl(I), and 𝒩cl(I)∕I inherits a Poisson bracket. The Leibniz and Jacobi identities prove closure under multiplication and bracket. Replacing one observable by a multiple of a constraint changes its bracket with another observable by an element of I. Representatives outside the normalizer can still define functions on a constraint surface as points; their brackets may depend on the choice of representative. For constraints generating a Hamiltonian action, reduction also asks that the restricted observable be constant on the gauge orbits. In the following examples the two descriptions agree explicitly.

3 A pair of boundary frames

Let (qL,pL) and (qR,pR) be the coordinates of T∗ℝ2, with {qj,pk} = δ𝑗𝑘. Both momenta refer to the outward orientation on their respective sides. Use the matching equation and frame transformation

n = pL + pR = 0,(qL,qR)⟼(qL + t,qR + t).

The transformation is generated by n with the convention Xnf = {f,n}. Its zero level sets the two outward charges equal and opposite. The sign depends on how the right frame is oriented.

Proposition 3.1. For R = ℂ[qL,qR,pL,pR] and I = (n), the classical reduced algebra is ℂ[r,k] with {r,k} = 1, where r = qL − qR and k = pL on the matching surface. The induced symplectic form on the real reduced space is 𝑑𝑟 ∧ 𝑑𝑘 in the convention ω = ∑ ⁡ jdqj ∧ dpj.

Proof. Set s = qR. In the new coordinates the canonical one-form is

pLdqL + pRdqR = k𝑑𝑟 + n𝑑𝑠.

Thus (r,k) and (s,n) are independent canonical pairs, and Xn = ∂s. At n = 0 the restricted symplectic form has kernel spanned by ∂s. Each orbit meets the surface s = 0 once. An element of R∕(n) = ℂ[r,k,s] is invariant under every real translation exactly when it is independent of s. The same condition is ∂sa ∈ (n) before taking the quotient, hence 𝒩cl(I)∕I = ℂ[r,k]. The bracket and form on the slice follow from the canonical one-form. □

A measurement before reduction illustrates what the normalizer discards. The single reading qL = s + r takes different values along the same orbit. Its difference from qR survives. The charge pR also survives, since it is − k at the fixed level. A separate reduction on each side by the full translation of its frame, with each moment map fixed at zero, sets both charges to zero. Each singly reduced phase space is a point. Combining these points loses the canonical pair (r,k) that the diagonal reduction retains. The choice of a common action has measurable content in the model.

A subgroup that preserves a boundary condition can still act on the reduced observables. A shift of the left reading alone induces r↦r + a and fixes k. It preserves the matching surface and commutes with the diagonal shifts. Its generator on the quotient is k. Declaring it gauge would impose a further reduction. This is the algebraic reason to identify both the null directions of the restricted presymplectic form and the charged boundary symmetries before constructing any algebra of a subregion.

4 Regular and repeated constraints

For a polynomial algebra R and a list of functions f = (f1,…,fm), use the cohomological convention for the Koszul complex

K(R;f) = R ⊗Λ(e1,…,em),|ei| = −1,dei = fi.

The differential vanishes on R and satisfies the graded Leibniz rule. Its zeroth cohomology is R∕(f1,…,fm). It computes the derived zero fiber of the specified map to affine m-space: resolve the origin there by its free coordinate Koszul resolution and pull that resolution back to R. Two maps with different targets can have the same ordinary zero set and different derived zero fibers. More general excess intersections have global geometric obstructions; the calculation below uses global linear coordinates [5].

Proposition 4.1. The complex K(R;n) for the surface in Proposition 3.1 has cohomology R∕(n) in degree zero and vanishing cohomology in negative degrees. The complex for the map given by (n,n) has

H0 = R∕(n),H−1 = (R∕(n))𝜀,Hj = 0(j < −1).

Here 𝜀 is represented by e2 − e1.

Proof. Multiplication by the coordinate n is injective, so the two-term complex Re1 → R resolves R∕(n). In the second complex set u = e1 and v = e2 − e1. Then 𝑑𝑢 = n and 𝑑𝑣 = 0. As a complex this is a copy of K(R;n) together with its shift by the degree of v. The class [v] survives because a boundary in its summand has coefficient divisible by n. □

The negative-degree class here remembers a relation between two tests of the same charge. It has no interpretation as a new radiative polarization. If a boundary problem presents one equation twice merely as a bookkeeping convention, the intended derived fiber uses the independent equation. Keeping the doubled target defines a different derived problem. Equivalences of presentations in a derived formulation must come with quasi-isomorphisms. The dimensions above rule one out between these two complexes.

The source of a stabilizer is a separate choice. For the single effective ℝ-action in Proposition 3.1, every orbit is free, including the orbits on the repeated zero locus. One can instead declare a group ℝ2 to act by shifting both frames by t1 + t2. Its subgroup {(t,−t)} fixes every point. That group action and the map with two identical constraints arise together if the moment map is defined for the whole redundant Lie algebra. The stabilizer and the Koszul class then have related dimensions but occupy different parts of the reduction. A quotient by the effective group removes the ineffective subgroup. Changing the derived target removes the redundant equation.

One can also have a stabilizer with independent constraint equations in other settings. For example, quotient the one-pair reduced plane by the finite symmetry (r,k)↦(−r,−k), without adding any Hamiltonian constraint. The origin has isotropy of order two. Its invariant functions u = r2, v = 𝑟𝑘, and w = k2 satisfy 𝑢𝑤 = v2. To see that they generate, take any monomial rakb with a + b even and group factors into pairs. The quotient surface is singular at the origin. Its finite stabilizer arises without repeating a single equation in the Koszul calculation.

5 Quantum reduction at a fixed level

Fix a real ℏ≠0. Let 𝒜 be the polynomial Weyl algebra over ℂ generated by R0,K,S,N, with [R0,K] = [S,N] = 𝑖ℏ1 and all commutators between the two pairs zero. Uppercase letters indicate operators. The antilinear involution fixes these four generators. Denote by B the Weyl subalgebra generated by R0,K. Classical variables can be recovered as principal commutative symbols in the formal-parameter family described below.

The left ideal L = 𝒜N contains the operators which visibly annihilate a vector satisfying 𝑁𝜓 = 0, on a common invariant domain when such vectors exist. Acting by a representative of an observable must preserve the constraint on states. Define the right normalizer of the left ideal by

𝒩(L) = {a ∈𝒜 : 𝐿𝑎 ⊂ L}.

Since L is a left ideal, L ⊂𝒩(L) and it is a two-sided ideal inside 𝒩(L). Thus the quotient 𝒩(L)∕L has a product. The algebraic definition continues to make sense when solutions to the state constraint are distributions.

Theorem 5.1. The inclusion B↪𝒩(L) induces an isomorphism of associative algebras

B →≃𝒩(L)∕L.

The same quotient has a presentation as a star algebra by the centralizer of N modulo the ideal generated by N inside that centralizer.

Proof. Normal ordering puts every element uniquely in the form

a = ∑ j,ℓ≥0b𝑗ℓSjNℓ,b 𝑗ℓ ∈ B,

with finitely many terms. The ordered basis follows by commuting N past S using the scalar commutator, or by acting with multiplication and differentiation on polynomials in the two coordinates. In the differential representation the highest nonvanishing derivative coefficient detects a nonzero normally ordered operator in characteristic zero. Terms with ℓ > 0 span L. Modulo L there is a unique representative a0(S) = ∑ ⁡ jbj0Sj.

It suffices to check 𝑁𝑎 ∈ L in the definition of 𝒩(L). Since 𝑎𝑁 ∈ L, this is equivalent to [N,a] ∈ L. Its class is the class of − 𝑖ℏ∂Sa0(S). The derivative vanishes exactly on constant polynomials, giving surjectivity from B. Its intersection with L is zero by the ordered basis, giving injectivity.

The centralizer 𝒵(N) contains exactly the terms independent of S, by differentiating their coefficients before taking any quotient. Thus 𝒵(N) = B[N] and 𝒵(N) ∩ L = B[N]N. This ideal is star stable there, and evaluation at N = 0 identifies the star quotient with B. Its image in 𝒩(L)∕L is the associative isomorphism just proved. □

A two-sided ideal of the full 𝒜 containing N contains [S,N] = 𝑖ℏ1. At the chosen nonzero value of ℏ, the quotient by that ideal vanishes. Also L itself fails star stability since 𝑆𝑁 ∈ L while (𝑆𝑁)∗ = 𝑁𝑆 = 𝑆𝑁 − 𝑖ℏ has nonzero constant class. The physical star from the centralizer thus cannot be computed by taking an arbitrary normalizer representative, applying the kinematical star and passing to its class. The proof specifies which observables carry a well-defined adjoint.

Let Bℏ now denote the polynomial algebra over ℂ[ℏ] with the same relative pair and relation [R0,K] = 𝑖ℏ. Normal ordering gives a free module with basis R0aKb. Its specialization at ℏ = 0 is the reduced Poisson polynomial algebra as a commutative algebra, with bracket obtained from [ ⋅, ⋅]∕(𝑖ℏ) followed by specialization. The coefficient of ℏ satisfies the Leibniz rule and has value one on the ordered generators. This proves the correspondence for all polynomials. In contrast, specializing the kinematical commutator condition [N,a] = 0 at ℏ = 0 before dividing removes the gauge derivative completely. The action or its divided derivation is part of the classical data.

The fixed-parameter calculation avoids the general analytic questions of quantization and reduction. Bordemann, Herbig and Waldmann derive hypotheses under which their quantum BRST reduction deforms the full classical reduced function algebra, and exhibit examples in which its degree-zero cohomology has different behavior [6]. The calculation here has a global gauge coordinate, constant commutators and a polynomial ordering. In gravity one has to establish which replacement for each assumption is available.

6 Linear boundary actions

The paired-frame calculation extends to several matching equations. Extra equations may depend on the others. Their dependence becomes a consistency test when choosing the quantum value of a charge.

Let V and U be real vector spaces of dimensions d and m. A linear map T : U → V sends gauge parameters to frame shifts. Its rank is a. On T∗V let a vector u ∈ U act by q↦q + 𝑇𝑢 with p fixed. The moment map μ : T∗V → U∗ satisfies

μ(q,p)(u) = p(𝑇𝑢).

Write H = im ⁡ T, F = ker⁡T. The action factors through the effective additive group H. A character value λ ∈ U∗ determines the level μ = λ. A nonzero λ is allowed even though the group is abelian. Physically it fixes what the matching prescription is to count as zero total charge.

Let 𝒜ℏ(V ) be the polynomial Weyl algebra with position Qξ linear in ξ ∈ V ∗ and momentum Pv linear in v ∈ V . Take [Qξ,Pv] = 𝑖ℏξ(v)1, with commuting positions and commuting momenta. All real generators are self-adjoint. Work over the complex numbers with real λ and fixed nonzero real ℏ. Define

Cλ(u) = P𝑇𝑢 − λ(u)1,Lλ = ∑ u∈U𝒜ℏ(V )Cλ(u).

Only a basis of U is needed in the sum. The constraints commute. Their effective translation action is free even when F is nonzero, because a nonzero translation of V fixes no point.

Theorem 6.1. The level is nonempty exactly when λ|F = 0. For an inconsistent level Lλ = 𝒜ℏ(V ). For a consistent level, after a choice of complement to H the following descriptions hold.

  1. The classical reduced phase space for the effective group is symplectomorphic to T∗(V∕H).
  2. The cohomology of the Koszul complex of the m component equations is the ring of the unreduced level tensored with Λ∙Fℂ, placing Fℂ in degree − 1. Here Fℂ is the complexification of F. Its bth exterior power contributes in degree − b for 0 ≤ b ≤ m − a.
  3. The quantum normalizer quotient 𝒩(Lλ)∕Lλ is a Weyl algebra on d − a canonical pairs. Its star structure comes from the joint centralizer of the constrained momenta.

Proof. A point on the level must satisfy p ∘ T = λ, so λ must vanish on F. Conversely such a λ defines a functional on H by 𝑇𝑢↦λ(u), which extends to V . For u ∈ F, the corresponding quantum constraint is − λ(u)1. If a nonzero scalar occurs, the ideal contains 1.

For a consistent level choose splittings V = H ⊕ W and U = U0 ⊕ F. Use coordinates sj on H and rb on W, with momenta nj,kb in the corresponding dual bases. The map T|U0 is invertible onto H. In a basis adapted to this map the first a constraints fix the momenta nj at numbers λj, and the last m − a vanish identically. The symplectic form restricts to ∑ ⁡ bdrb ∧ dkb. The quotient by translations in s leaves the stated cotangent bundle.

The shifted coordinates nj − λj form a regular sequence. In fact the constraint complex factors over ℂ into independent two-term coordinate complexes, each exact in degree − 1, a polynomial ring in the other variables, and an exterior algebra with zero differential on the generators in Fℂ. The cohomology follows by tensoring over a field. When taking invariants under the effective translations afterward, the polynomial dependence on the sj drops out. This last observation concerns ordinary invariants of the displayed cohomology. A general derived quotient by a group requires its own cohomological construction.

For the quantum algebra put every Nj − λj at the far right of each normally ordered monomial. The terms containing any such factor span Lλ. Modulo Lλ, each class has a unique representative polynomial in S1,…,Sa with coefficients in the residual Weyl algebra. Normalizing all the constraints requires each of its partial derivatives in Sj to vanish. Thus just the constant coefficient remains. The centralizer consists of residual Weyl polynomials together with polynomials in the commuting Nj. Evaluation Nj = λj on this centralizer is a star map because the levels are real. □

At λ = 0 the quotient identification needs fewer choices. The momentum p then belongs to Ann ⁡ (H) ≃ (V∕H)∗, and a gauge orbit of q specifies a point of V∕H. At a nonzero consistent level the allowed momenta form an affine translate of Ann ⁡ (H). Choosing an extension of the character from H to V identifies this affine space with a vector space. The theorem makes no preferred choice among such extensions. A comparison of different diamonds could depend on this choice through its action on momenta.

There is a useful direct form of the affine choice problem. Suppose the same boundary level is expressed by two real-valued linear functionals on V , p∗ and p∗′. Both extend the fixed character on H. Their difference 𝜃 annihilates H, hence defines a cotangent vector on the quotient. The change of origin of the reduced momentum acts by k↦k − 𝜃. In the quantum Weyl algebra such a real shift preserves commutators and the adjoint. A named observable interpreted as a fixed absolute momentum nevertheless changes under the shift. When physical preparation data fix a preferred zero, the comparison must carry that zero along with the ring operations.

7 Infinitesimal gauge cochains

A derived zero fiber still carries the gauge action. For translations both operations can be examined in one complex. The following calculation also shows what it costs to retain an ineffective gauge generator. It uses polynomial Lie algebra cochains for an abelian Lie algebra. Other choices, including equivariant topological cohomology, ask different cohomological questions.

Take a consistent character and the adapted coordinates of Theorem 6.1. Write R for the ring of complex polynomials in (r,k,s,n). Introduce a degree − 1 generator for each component of the moment map as above. Let the entire abelian Lie algebra U act by its given translation on position coordinates and trivially on momentum coordinates and Koszul generators. Form the Chevalley and Eilenberg cochains of this action on the Koszul algebra. The new degree + 1 generators are linear functions on U. These generators anticommute with the odd Koszul generators under the usual total-degree sign rule.

In a basis of U0 mapped to the coordinate translations of H, write ej for the active Koszul generators and cj for the ghosts dual to those translations. In a basis of F write vi for its Koszul generators and wi for the additional Lie algebra cochains. The full differential D satisfies

Dej = nj − λj,Dsj = cj,Dvi = Dwi = Dcj = Dnj = 0. (1)

It vanishes on r,k. For homogeneous x of total degree |x| (Koszul degree plus Lie cochain degree), its value on a product is D(𝑥𝑦) = (𝐷𝑥)y + (−1)|x|x(𝐷𝑦). The two component differentials are compatible because the constraints are invariant under translations. In particular D2 = 0 on each generator. Nilpotence alone does not identify the cohomology with the algebra of ordinary observables.

Proposition 7.1. The cohomology of the polynomial complex (1) is

ℂ[r,k] ⊗Λ∙Fℂ ⊗Λ∙Fℂ∗,

with degrees − 1 and + 1 on the two exterior generating spaces, respectively. Each term ΛiFℂ ⊗ΛjFℂ∗ contributes in total degree j − i. With independent constraints and the effective group, this cohomology is concentrated in degree zero and equals the ordinary reduced observable algebra.

Proof. For each j the subcomplex with variables nj − λj,ej is the two-term coordinate resolution in Proposition 4.1. The other factor with variables sj,cj is the polynomial complex

ℂ[sj] →∂sjℂ[sj]cj.

Its kernel consists of constants. In characteristic zero every polynomial coefficient has a polynomial antiderivative, so the derivative is onto. Both factors have just ℂ in degree zero as cohomology. Taking tensor products over a field gives the assertion, since the residual polynomial variables, vi and wi all have zero differential. □

When dim⁡F = 1 there is a class v in degree − 1 from the redundant equation and a class w in degree + 1 from the ineffective Lie algebra generator. The product 𝑣𝑤 is a nonzero class of total degree zero with (𝑣𝑤)2 = 0. Thus even degree-zero cohomology can remember a pair of extra choices in this complex. Dropping the ineffective Lie algebra and keeping the doubled equation deletes w but retains v. Replacing both inputs by the effective ones deletes both classes. The reduced ordinary observable algebras in Theorem 6.1 are the ones obtained by ordinary effective reduction. This distinction prevents an inference from a correctly computed cohomology group to a statement about the physical observables it should represent.

The proof uses an explicit primitive in the orbit coordinate. That primitive need not satisfy the boundary conditions of another problem. For comparison, replace one noncompact coordinate s by an angular coordinate and act on smooth periodic functions. The derivative of a periodic function has integral zero around the circle, and any smooth zero-integral function has a periodic primitive. The constant coefficient of a one-form therefore gives a one-dimensional cokernel. The orbit action is still free. This degree-one class reflects the cochain complex chosen on a compact orbit. It supplies a second reason to specify the coefficient space and the treatment of boundary conditions in a gravitational reduction.

8 A constraint of changing rank

The rank hypothesis of Theorem 6.1 can fail along a family of boundary data. The simplest example uses the same coordinates (r,k,s,n) as the paired-frame model. Introduce a real parameter τ which the Poisson bracket holds fixed. A parameter u ∈ ℝ acts on s by s↦s + 𝜏𝑢. Its moment map is

fτ = 𝜏𝑛. (2)

For each τ≠0 the ordinary zero fiber has n = 0, and translating s removes a frame coordinate. At τ = 0 every point satisfies the equation and the effective action is trivial. Reduction of this individual fiber leaves two canonical pairs. Extending results for the regular fibers to the exceptional value would miss a whole canonical pair.

8.1 The relative zero fiber

Over the polynomial parameter ring take D = ℂ[τ,r,k,s,n]. The family of ordinary zero fibers has coordinate ring D∕(𝜏𝑛). This ring has τ-torsion: the nonzero class of n is killed by τ. Thus this ordinary family is not flat over ℂ[τ]. There is a simple geometric explanation. Its zero set has a component n = 0 over every value of the parameter, and an additional component, with n arbitrary, lying entirely over τ = 0.

The equation 𝜏𝑛 is a nonzero divisor in D. Its Koszul complex

𝐷𝑒 →𝜏𝑛D

therefore has no negative-degree cohomology over the whole family. After setting τ = 0 in the complex, its differential vanishes. The exceptional fiber has a copy of ℂ[r,k,s,n] in degrees 0 and − 1. This calculation uses specialization of the complex of free ℂ[τ]-modules. In particular there is more information in that specialization than in tensoring the cohomology groups with ℂ[τ]∕(τ) after computing them on the total space.

One can see the additional term without introducing Koszul generators. Use the two-term resolution of ℂ[τ]∕(τ) whose differential is multiplication by τ. Tensor it with D∕(𝜏𝑛). The kernel of the resulting differential is the ideal (n) in D∕(𝜏𝑛). Multiplication by n identifies ℂ[r,k,s,n] with that kernel, because every term containing both τ and n vanishes and every surviving multiple of n has a unique polynomial coefficient at τ = 0. This identifies the first Tor contribution with the degree − 1 class in the specialized Koszul model. Thus the surplus class can be traced either to a dependent equation in an individual fiber or to failure of flatness in a family.

The quantum discrepancy can be computed without any homological language. Keep ℏ real and nonzero and set A0 = 𝒜ℏ(ℝ2) in the variables of Theorem 5.1. For polynomials in τ let

A = A0[τ],L = 𝐴𝜏𝑁,M = {a ∈ A0 : A0𝑁𝑎 ⊂ A0N}.

The rank-changing normalizer is defined using the single family of constraints. Reducing instead after specializing the kinematical algebra uses the individual constraint f0 = 0.

Proposition 8.1. The specialized map from the family normalizer quotient to the physical quotient of the exceptional fiber is the strict inclusion

(𝒩(L)∕L)/τ(𝒩(L)∕L) ≃M↪A0.

For τ0≠0, specialization at τ0 gives the one-pair Weyl algebra B.

Proof. It suffices to test the generator 𝜏𝑁 in the normalizer condition. Since τ is central and is a nonzero divisor in A, the relation 𝜏𝑁𝑎 ∈ 𝜏𝐴𝑁 is equivalent to 𝑁𝑎 ∈ 𝐴𝑁. The latter holds coefficient by coefficient in τ. Hence 𝒩(L) = M[τ]. The ordered-monomial calculation in Theorem 5.1 gives M = B + A0N as vector subspaces, so A0N ⊂ M. It follows that L ⊂ 𝜏𝑀[τ]. The special fiber of M[τ]∕L is M.

The constraint in the exceptional kinematical fiber generates the zero ideal of A0. Its normalizer is all of A0. The induced map is the inclusion M → A0, which misses S because 𝑁𝑆 = 𝑆𝑁 − 𝑖ℏ lies outside A0N. At τ = τ0≠0 the image of L is A0N since τ0 is invertible, giving M∕A0N = B. □

Two observations make the strict inclusion visible on states. In a regular fiber the wavefunctions solving the translation constraint have a fixed N-value and a free R0 argument. At the exceptional fiber a normalized kinematical packet can depend on both r and s. Multiplication by S distinguishes two packets translated in s. There is no family of polynomial normalizer representatives whose value at zero is this operator. Also a representative proportional to N can survive at τ = 0 in the source algebra M, even though at a regular level it vanishes in the quotient. Both effects follow from performing different operations in a different order. The model requires no limit of Hilbert spaces as τ approaches zero.

A regular family of null diamonds likewise needs a stated rank condition if its reduced phase spaces are to have a common description. Equation (2) is an example of what to test when that condition fails. It gives no basis for assigning this singularity to a caustic or marginal surface in a spacetime solution. Such an assignment would require the linearized constraints of that particular solution and their boundary conditions.

9 Matching around a cycle

A number assigned to each cut may look consistent when two neighboring pieces are examined and fail when the cuts close into a loop. The linear theorem turns this into a calculation.

Let G = (V G,EG) be a finite oriented graph with c connected components. Retain isolated vertices. Put one frame position and one momentum at each vertex and assign a translation parameter to each edge. The column of T : ℝEG → ℝV G for the edge e : v → w is the vector ew − ev. The corresponding constraint fixes pw − pv = λe. Reversing the edge reverses the sign of its column and of λe. This is a charge-difference convention; the two-boundary convention of Section 3 reverses the momentum on one side.

Corollary 9.1. The vertex matching problem has a nonzero quantum quotient exactly when the signed sum of λe around every cycle vanishes. In that case the quotient has c canonical pairs. The unreduced derived level has degree − 1 Koszul cohomology free over the ordinary level ring of rank b = |EG|−|V G| + c. Its successive exterior powers fill degrees − j for 0 ≤ j ≤ b.

Proof. The transpose of the incidence matrix maps a vector of vertex values p to its edge differences. Such differences have vanishing sum around every cycle. Conversely choose one root in each component. Set a vertex value to the signed sum of the edge assignments along any path from the root. Vanishing cycle sums give path independence. A constant per component remains free, hence dim⁡ker⁡T∗ = c and rank ⁡ T = |V G|− c. Apply Theorem 6.1. □

In a triangle with arrows 1 → 2 → 3 → 1, fix λ12 + λ23 + λ31 = 0. One may solve the constraints by p1 = t, p2 = t + λ12 and p3 = t + λ12 + λ23. The sum Q = q1 + q2 + q3 is invariant under all edge shifts. Its conjugate can be taken to be K = (p1 + p2 + p3)∕3. On this level K = t + (2λ12 + λ23)∕3, so it differs from the free momentum t by a fixed offset. Indeed {Q,K} = 1 and the quantized operators satisfy [Q,K] = 𝑖ℏ. The Koszul class represented by e12 + e23 + e31 is closed. It has degree − 1 and spans the excess part over the ring of the unreduced level. Thus the classical observable pair, the quantum observable pair and the redundant matching equation can each be named in the same example.

Now put λ12 = λ23 = 0 and λ31 = ℏ𝛼 with real α≠0. Every proper two-edge subgraph of the triangle has consistent levels. Adding the third edge gives

Cλ(e12) + Cλ(e23) + Cλ(e31) = −ℏ𝛼1.

The full left ideal contains a nonzero scalar and the quotient vanishes. At ℏ = 0 all three level assignments reduce to the ordinary unshifted matching. This is an obstruction to lifting those particular levels together, despite consistency on each tree. It is an algebraic test of an assignment of charge offsets. Calling it a gravitational anomaly would require an invariant construction of the offsets in gravity and proof that no allowed redefinition removes it.

A change of vertex momentum origins pv↦pv + bv changes the edge character by bw − bv. The signed sum around any cycle stays fixed. Conversely Corollary 9.1 identifies all removable assignments as vertex differences. A spanning tree gives an economical measurement: calibrate vertex origins along the tree, then measure each remaining edge once. If an edge disagrees, no new vertex calibration can satisfy that edge while retaining every tree match. In the formal-parameter algebra a nonzero scalar multiple of ℏ implies ℏ-torsion rather than instant collapse. Inverting ℏ or specializing to a nonzero numerical value gives the zero quotient of the example.

The dimension c counts components of this chosen graph of gauge translations. It gives no degree-of-freedom count for a gravitational partition of spacetime. The assumptions making the count possible are that each frame variable is one-dimensional, translations commute, constraints are linear and their only dependencies are graph cycles. A nonabelian charge matching problem instead needs a specified composition law for changes of frame, and representation data can distinguish sectors with the same classical label.

10 Transport of a constraint ideal

The arrows of a putative diamond category must do more than relate the unconstrained algebras. For algebras 𝒜j with left ideals Lj, j = 1,2, suppose a unital algebra map α : 𝒜1 →𝒜2 satisfies

α(L1) ⊂ L2, (3) α(𝒩(L1)) ⊂𝒩(L2). (4)

Then [a]↦[α(a)] induces a unital algebra map of normalizer quotients. Equation (3) gives independence of representatives. Equation (4) ensures the image represents an observable. These conditions compose. If each physical adjoint comes from a star-stable centralizer presentation, a sufficient additional requirement is that α act as a star map on the centralizer and preserve its chosen target centralizer. An embedding of unconstrained algebras by itself establishes none of the conditions concerning target constraints.

A stronger hypothesis reduces the number of separate checks. Suppose α(L1) ⊂ L2 and the left ideal generated by α(L1) in 𝒜2 equals L2. If a ∈𝒩(L1) and b = ∑ ⁡ iciα(ℓi) ∈ L2, then

bα(a) = ∑ iciα(ℓia) ∈ L2.

Here multiplicativity of α allows each product to pass inside, and ℓia ∈ L1 because a normalizes the left ideal. Thus equation (4) holds. An enlarged boundary can acquire independent constraints, in which case this sufficient hypothesis may fail. The original normalizer test still makes sense.

For a simple obstruction let αt act on the two-pair algebra by N↦N + 𝑡ℏ and fix R0,K,S, with t ∈ ℝ. It preserves the star and all commutators. This formula also defines a map over ℂ[ℏ], and its specialization at ℏ = 0 is the identity on all four generators. If both regions impose N = 0 at the quantum level, αt(N) lies in 𝒜N exactly when t = 0. A reduced representative and the same representative plus N then have images differing by 𝑡ℏ modulo the target ideal. For t≠0, equation (3) fails and there is no map induced by this rule on all representatives.

The abstract physical algebras in the shifted example are nevertheless isomorphic. They have common representatives in B, on which αt is the identity. One can either use that particular section to define a different prescription, or change the target constraint to N + 𝑡ℏ = 0, in which case the original map does respect the chosen levels. The distinction is an experimentally meaningful one when levels and observables have fixed operational definitions. A null-cone correspondence must specify the frame charge detected by a preparation before treating changes of origin as equivalent.

11 Physical states at a noncompact boundary

An algebraic quotient does not automatically furnish a subspace of normalized physical vectors. In the real-coordinate representation on L2(ℝ2,𝑑𝑟𝑑𝑠) take initially the Schwartz domain and let

R0 = r,K = −𝑖ℏ∂r,S = s,N = −𝑖ℏ∂s.

The constraint would set a state independent of s. Any such function in L2(ℝ2) has zero norm or infinite norm in the s-direction, so the zero eigenspace of the self-adjoint momentum N contains only the zero vector. The reduced Weyl algebra in Theorem 5.1 still has its usual Schrödinger representation on L2(ℝ,𝑑𝑟). One must account for the gauge orbit when comparing the state spaces.

For Schwartz wavefunctions, consider the map

(𝜂𝜓)(r) = ∫ ℝψ(r,s)𝑑𝑠.

Its normalization fixes a choice of measure on the gauge group. Integration by parts gives η(𝑁𝜓) = 0, and R0,K commute through this averaging map on the common domain. Every Schwartz function f(r) has a preimage f(r)h(s) with h Schwartz and ∫ h(s)𝑑𝑠 = 1. The positive semidefinite form

(ψ,ϕ)red = ∫ ℝ(𝜂𝜓)(r)¯(𝜂𝜙)(r)𝑑𝑟

therefore has quotient completion L2(ℝ,𝑑𝑟). This identifies the physical scalar product for the model by a construction that can be checked on representatives. It does not turn the average into a bounded projection on the kinematical Hilbert space.

For example choose a Schwartz function h with nonzero integral and set hb(s) = b−1∕2h(s∕b) for b > 0. Its kinematical norm is constant while ∫ hb = b∫ h. For a fixed nonzero f(r), the norms of η(fhb) tend to infinity. No bounded extension of η from the full kinematical Hilbert space can have this action on the Schwartz domain. Replacing the gauge average by an ordinary Hilbert projection would give a different answer.

A real level λ changes the test functional to

(ηλψ)(r) = ∫ ℝe−𝑖𝜆𝑠∕ℏψ(r,s)𝑑𝑠.

Then ηλ((N − λ)ψ) = 0 by integration by parts. A multiplication operator Utψ = e𝑖𝑡𝑠ψ satisfies NUt = Ut(N + 𝑡ℏ) and ηλ+𝑡ℏ(Utψ) = ηλ(ψ). The phase implements a change of level provided the physical state prescription changes along with the operator. The quantum shift that obstructs descent at two fixed equal levels now has a precise intertwiner between two different levels.

These arguments separate three assertions about a proposed local quantum system. A polynomial star algebra may admit a representation on a dense domain. A bounded algebra of observables may be generated by exponentiating suitable self-adjoint operators. A von Neumann algebra depends on the selected representation and its closure. A theorem about a star-algebra quotient cannot by itself identify the latter two. Chandrasekaran specifies deformations of the action and a positive state in constructing his representation [1, §§4.1, 4.2]. An independent categorical construction must identify the comparable operators, the state, and any limiting operation used to form a closure.

12 A state comparison on the relative pair

A comparison can preserve the constraint algebra and still assign different probabilities to a boundary measurement. The relative pair admits a direct calculation of those probabilities. Work in the Schrödinger representation of Section 5, after group averaging has removed s. For real a,b define a bounded unitary on L2(ℝ,𝑑𝑟) by

(W(a,b)f)(r) = exp⁡(𝑖𝑎(r + b∕2)∕ℏ)f(r + b). (5)

Here a has units of momentum and b has units of position. Translation preserves Lebesgue measure, so W(a,b) is indeed unitary. Direct multiplication gives

W(a,b)W(c,d) = exp⁡( − i(𝑎𝑑 − 𝑏𝑐)∕(2ℏ))W(a + c,b + d).

It follows that W(a,b)∗ = W(−a,−b). These are bounded probes of the pair. Their product is meaningful for every Hilbert vector, in contrast with a high polynomial moment of an unbounded generator. The symmetrically ordered characteristic function of displacement operators also appears in the phase-space treatment of Cahill and Glauber [7].

For a unit vector ψ, write χψ(a,b) = ⟨ψ,W(a,b)ψ⟩. An algebra of measured observables may be defined as the operator norm closure of the finite linear span of these W(a,b) in this representation. Equality of two states on all the displacements implies equality on that algebra by linearity and norm continuity. For the calculation it suffices to work with normalized vectors and the finite linear span.

Proposition 12.1. Let σ > 0 and x,p ∈ ℝ. The normalized packet

gx,p,σ(r) = (2πσ2)−1∕4 exp⁡ (−(r − x)2 4σ2 + 𝑖𝑝(r − x) ℏ )

has characteristic function

χx,p,σ(a,b) = exp⁡ (i(𝑎𝑥 + 𝑏𝑝) ℏ − σ2a2 2ℏ2 − b2 8σ2 ) . (6)

Equality for all a,b distinguishes the three parameters within this family, up to the irrelevant common phase of a wavefunction.

Proof. The squared modulus is the normal probability density with mean x and variance σ2. In the integral for χ the product of the two wavefunctions has the factor e𝑖𝑝𝑏∕ℏ. Put u = r − x + b∕2. Completing the square gives

(r − x)2 + (r + b − x)2 = 2u2 + b2∕2.

The remaining phase in (5) is e𝑖𝑎(u+x)∕ℏ. Integrating e𝑖𝑎𝑢∕ℏ against the centered normal density yields e−σ2a2∕(2ℏ2) , proving (6). The first derivatives at the origin recover x,p; the modulus along the a-axis determines σ2. Positivity of the width fixes its square root. □

A chosen pair of preparations can now test an affine change of boundary reference. For displacements let

αδ,𝜖(W(a,b)) = ei(𝑎𝛿+𝑏𝜖)∕ℏW(a,b).

The scalar is a character of the additive parameter space. It respects products and adjoints. The map is unitarily implemented in this representation by conjugation with a displacement, as follows also from the product law. The underlying operator prescription corresponds to R0↦R0 + δ and K↦K + 𝜖.

Let the first observer use parameters (x1,p1,σ1) and the second use (x2,p2,σ2). Equality of the first state with the pullback of the second then holds on the displacement algebra exactly when

x1 = x2 + δ,p1 = p2 + 𝜖,σ1 = σ2. (7)

Indeed (6) gives these conditions and proves their sufficiency. A mismatch of means can be accounted for by an identified offset in the frame origin. A mismatch of widths remains after such a translation. Two preparations with zero means and different widths agree on the means of both generators and on the canonical commutator, yet give different values even for Re ⁡ χ(a,0) at every a≠0. Thus a second moment can separate comparisons that a measurement of charge and position means cannot distinguish.

Rescaling the unit of the frame reading changes the test. For γ > 0 take αγ(W(a,b)) = W(𝛾𝑎,b∕γ). The product phase in (5) is unchanged, and dilation of the Schrödinger coordinate implements this star automorphism. Pulling back the second Gaussian state gives parameters (γx2,p2∕γ,γσ2). To use the width as a test one must fix the physical normalization of the probe or include its prescribed rescaling. This dependence is relevant for null frames, where a convention for normalizing the null normals enters a comparison of readings.

Agreement on one commutative family of probes can also fail to identify a state. The packets gx,p,σ and gx,p′,σ for distinct p,p′ have identical probability distributions for R0. Every bounded function of R0 therefore has the same expectation in the two states. A translation probe with a = 0 has expectation e𝑖𝑏𝑝∕ℏ−b2∕(8σ2) and distinguishes the states for any b with b(p − p′)∕ℏ outside 2𝜋ℤ. In a boundary measurement this distinction asks which noncommuting operations the two constructions allow a preparation to undergo. Knowing every probability for the charge alone would still give only the diagonal spectral data of that charge.

The measurements in this section can be made after gauge averaging on representatives. For a normalized residual packet g choose any Schwartz function h with ∫ h(s)𝑑𝑠 = 1. Applying the bounded displacement in the r variable to g(r)h(s) before applying η produces W(a,b)g. Replacing h by any other function with the same integral changes that kinematical representative but leaves every residual displacement expectation the same. For a nonzero level the corresponding condition is ∫ e−𝑖𝜆𝑠∕ℏh(s)𝑑𝑠 = 1. Thus equality of the probability tests is compatible with the level-dependent state prescription, not just with a preferred gauge slice.

13 Translations implemented up to phase

An algebra map can respect products on the nose when the operators implementing a boundary symmetry respect composition only up to a phase. This issue is independent of the level obstruction in Corollary 9.1. In that example the global quotient vanished. The following example has a nonzero Hilbert space and an exact action on every bounded observable.

On the reduced Hilbert space define U(a)f(r) = f(r − a) and V (b)f(r) = e𝑖𝑏𝑟∕ℏf(r). Both shift the surviving phase-space data. They are boundary operations on the surviving canonical pair and do not impose two additional gauge conditions. Direct substitution gives

U(a)V (b) = e−𝑖𝑎𝑏∕ℏV (b)U(a). (8)

The scalar disappears in the inner automorphisms A↦U(a)𝐴𝑈(a)∗ and A↦V (b)𝐴𝑉 (b)∗. These automorphisms commute even when the two unitaries anticommute. They preserve the residual displacement algebra since conjugating a displacement multiplies it by a character.

Proposition 13.1. Fix two nonzero steps a0,b0. The two commuting automorphisms associated with these steps define an action of ℤ2 on bounded operators. In the given representation there is a pair of implementing unitaries giving a true representation of ℤ2 if and only if a0b0∕(2𝜋ℏ) is an integer.

Proof. In the algebra of all bounded operators on the Hilbert space, unitaries implementing the same inner automorphism differ by a scalar. Indeed their quotient commutes with all bounded operators, including every rank-one operator, so it is a multiple of the identity. Changing scalar phases of U(a0),V (b0) leaves their group commutator unchanged. Its value is e−ia0b0∕ℏ1 by (8). A representation of the free abelian group on two generators requires that commutator to be one. When the condition holds, the assignment (m,n)↦U(a0)mV (b0)n gives the representation. □

A way to compute the obstruction without considering all elements of the group is to compare the two paths around the elementary square. For two intertwiners one first fixes the identifications of the endpoint Hilbert spaces. The product around the square is then central when it acts trivially by conjugation on the full operator algebra. For irreducible observables the center here is scalar, and the product of phases is therefore a number of unit modulus. A phase assigned to each endpoint cancels around a closed square. For (8) that number is exactly e−ia0b0∕ℏ.

The square also indicates which type of observation could detect the discrepancy in the model. Prepare a two-dimensional control in (|0⟩ + |1⟩)∕2 and a normalized residual state f. On branch zero act on f by the identity, and on branch one act by 𝑈𝑉 U∗V ∗ with the same specified implementations. The final control has components 1 and e−ia0b0∕ℏ, up to their common normalization. Projecting it back on its initial state gives probability

1 + cos⁡(a0b0∕ℏ) 2 .

On the residual system alone, every expectation value is unchanged on both branches. This calculation relies on a coherent implementation of the controlled operations; specifying just the two automorphisms would not fix such an experiment. In a gravitational application one would have to identify a preparation and a physically calibrated relative phase. A projective phase of an isolated state has no separately measured value.

The step area a0b0 can be arbitrarily small compared with ℏ while leaving a mathematically nontrivial phase. Repeating the loop m times changes the phase to e−𝑖𝑚a0b0∕ℏ. The accumulation law follows from taking a product of the same central element. It is a quantitative example of the difference between one local comparison of generators and a comparison of transition amplitudes under repeated composition. It also explains why a numerical verification of a single commuting square of algebra maps could consistently pass while an amplitude calculation disagrees.

A reducible representation requires a more careful interpretation of the square. Two implementers giving the same action on a represented algebra can differ by a unitary in its commutant. For instance represent the same Weyl pair on H ⊗ ℂm by operators W(a,b) ⊗ 1. Every 1 ⊗ M commutes with them. An implementation of a repeated operation can therefore act nontrivially on a multiplicity space even when it acts trivially on every measured displacement. The scalar proof above used the full bounded algebra deliberately. If a categorical description loses the multiplicity of a boundary preparation, identifying its local observable algebra will not recover this part of the state comparison.

Chandrasekaran encounters an explicit endpoint phase when passing between reference actions in his equation (4.3) and the discussion immediately following it [1]. There the change of convention also changes the cap wavefunction, allowing the endpoint phases to cancel in an amplitude. This mechanism calls for a rule on the states as well as on the algebra. A common rephasing of endpoints and a nontrivial scalar left by a closed comparison have different observational consequences, as the square calculation shows.

14 Matching a positive boost charge

There is a more direct contact with a gravitational calculation than an analogy between two canonical pairs. In Chandrasekaran’s Appendix D.2, equation (D.13) integrates the intermediate rapidity of a shared normal frame. With his dimensionless area label s = A∕(4GNℏ), a relative boost by Γ acts on that sector with phase eiΓs∕(2π) [1]. Matching two sectors with labels sL,sR leaves a phase depending on the difference of their rapidities. This formula tests three pieces of a prospective dictionary at once: its area convention, its orientation convention and its normalization of the integration variable.

To isolate the Fourier calculation, take continuum labels and test functions with compact support inside (0,∞)2. Suppose ΓL,ΓR are fixed external readings. Give the intermediate rapidity α a symmetric Gaussian window with scale B > 0. The corresponding kernel on the two label variables is

KB(sR,sL) = ∫ ℝ 𝑑𝛼 4π2 exp⁡ (− α2 2B2 ) exp⁡ ( i 2π[sR(ΓR − α) + sL(α −ΓL)]) = ei(sRΓR−sLΓL)∕(2π) B (2π)3∕2 exp⁡ (−B2(sL − sR)2 8π2 ) . (9)

The second line is the elementary Fourier transform of a Gaussian. In the difference variable the real factor is a normalized approximate identity of standard deviation 2π∕B. After integration against the test function, its limit is evaluation on the diagonal. Compact support away from zero permits extension by zero without any endpoint convention. The result is the distribution

K∞(sR,sL) = eisR(ΓR−ΓL)∕(2π)δ(s L − sR).

This reproduces the rapidity dependence and the normalization of the continuum Fourier identity in (D.13). The Gaussian window here is a way of testing the distributional limit. It is independent of a choice of Gaussian state for gravitational modes.

The result is sensitive to the normalization of the rapidity integral. If a construction integrates a variable β = 𝑐𝛼 with c > 0, it must transform both the exponent and the measure. Keeping the physical phases fixed but integrating with 𝑑𝛽∕(4π2) in place of 𝑑𝛼∕(4π2) multiplies the entire kernel by c. The sharp matching locus looks identical in both answers. Absolute amplitudes sewn with the two measures then disagree. Thus the equality of constraints sL = sR and equality of normalized sewing kernels are independently testable statements.

14.1 A comparison of spectral measures

For a self-adjoint, nonnegative area operator A^, a normalized state ρ gives its spectral probability measure μρ on [0,∞). The boost probe has expectation

ρ (eiΓA^∕(8πGNℏ)) = ∫ [0,∞)eiΓA∕(8πGNℏ)dμ ρ(A). (10)

Here the bounded functional calculus or a representation supporting it is understood. For a vector state, μρ(I) is the squared norm of the projection on the area interval I. Agreement of (10) for every real Γ, with common constants and rapidity convention, is equivalent to agreement of the probability measures. The forward direction follows from uniqueness of the Fourier transform of a finite measure. To see why, integrate the difference of their characteristic functions against the inverse Fourier transform of a smooth compactly supported test function. Fourier inversion and absolute integrability then give zero for the integral of every such test function against the measure difference. These functions determine a finite measure.

For a sharp area eigenstate a single prescribed rapidity produces a periodic ambiguity. At Γ0≠0, replacing A by A + 2π(8πGNℏ)j∕Γ0 for an integer j leaves that expectation unchanged whenever both labels are admissible. Two nonzero rapidities whose ratio is irrational remove this exact ambiguity for a sharp label, since equality of both phases would require the same nonzero area shift to be an integer period for both. For a general distribution two probes still provide only two Fourier samples. Verifying them cannot establish equality of all spectral probabilities. This distinction matters when an intended comparison specifies a small set of preparation amplitudes instead of an entire state.

Even full equality of the area marginal does not settle the quantum state. To see this without any domain subtleties, take H = L2(ℝ+,𝑑𝐴) ⊗ ℂ2 with the area acting by multiplication in the first factor. Fix a normalized packet f(A) and take the two vectors f ⊗|0⟩ and f ⊗|1⟩. Every boost and every bounded function of area have identical expectation in the two states. The operator 1 ⊗|0⟩⟨0| distinguishes them. The second factor represents an undetected label in this calculation. The gravitational proposal contains fluctuating radiative data within a fixed area in §4.2, so a comparison restricted to the boost subgroup would require a separate calculation on its radiative observables.

14.2 Continuous and atomic matching

A Dirac delta on a continuum of labels and a projector on equal eigenvalues of a discrete operator both express matching. They act on different test spaces. To compare them let D be the self-adjoint difference of the two area labels divided by 2π in a tensor product representation. The functions of area on opposite factors strongly commute. A normalized finite average of the corresponding one-parameter unitary group is

PT = 1 2T∫ −T T e𝑖𝛼𝐷𝑑𝛼,P T = sinc ⁡ (𝑇𝐷),sinc ⁡ (t) = { sin⁡ ⁡ (t)∕t,t≠0, 1, t = 0.

For any vector its scalar spectral measure for D is finite. The integrand multiplier has absolute value at most one, equals one at zero, and tends to zero at every nonzero point. Applying dominated convergence to the norm squared of the difference shows PT → ED({0}) strongly, where ED is the spectral resolution of D.

With pure point labels on each side this projection keeps vectors with equal labels along with all their multiplicities. For discrete eigenvalues the list of allowed pairs and their degeneracies can be computed before normalizing the physical trace. With an absolutely continuous joint label measure on the plane, the diagonal has measure zero, hence ED({0}) = 0. The normalized average then converges strongly to zero in the kinematical Hilbert space. The unnormalized distributional pairing in (9) can nevertheless be nonzero on smooth test functions. For example a smooth nonnegative bump positive at a diagonal point has a positive diagonal integral when ΓL = ΓR.

In the latter case the diagonal data need a physical scalar product obtained after reduction, just as η did for a noncompact frame position. Substituting a symbol δ(0) for the diagonal measure in a countable basis would conflate the two constructions. A proposed identification of boundary Hilbert spaces must specify whether it preserves a spectral subspace, a direct integral or the physical space completed after distributional matching. The Fourier example decides which question to ask once the spectral measures are given. It does not select a spectrum for the gravitational theory.

14.3 The range of the area label

The global range of a charge restricts the operations that can act on it. Suppose A^ is a self-adjoint nonnegative operator on a nonzero Hilbert space. If for every t ∈ ℝ a unitary V (t) obeyed

V (t)A^V (t)∗ = A^ + t1

as self-adjoint operators with their domains, then invariance of the spectrum under unitary conjugation would give spec ⁡ (A^) = spec ⁡ (A^) + t for every t. The only nonempty subset of ℝ invariant under every translation is ℝ itself. This contradicts nonnegativity. The assumption is a global unitary translation law. A commutator identity on a chosen test domain alone does not imply this law, a distinction studied in other semibounded canonical-pair problems by Galapon [8].

Accordingly the real Weyl pair of Section 5 cannot be identified globally with a positive area and a conjugate generating arbitrary additive shifts of that area. A local canonical coordinate can still describe fluctuations in a regular chart, and the boost itself acts by a phase at fixed area. These operations ask for substantially less than a global two-sided shift of the area spectrum.

A simple alternative illustrates the importance of the range. On L2((0,∞),𝑑𝐴∕A) let A^ multiply by A and define (V (t)f)(A) = f(etA). The invariant measure 𝑑𝐴∕A makes V (t) unitary. On the self-adjoint multiplication domain it gives V (t)A^V (t)∗ = etA^. Hence conjugating a boost by a dilation rescales its rapidity:

V (t)exp⁡(iΓA^∕(8πGNℏ))V (t)∗ = exp⁡(ietΓA^∕(8πG Nℏ)).

Here zero belongs to the spectrum as a limit point and is preserved by dilation. In logarithmic coordinates x = log⁡(A∕A∗) for a chosen positive reference area A∗, V (t) acts by an ordinary translation of x. This model gives a consistent pair of operations preserving positivity. It is a comparison example; the gravitational admissibility of a dilation would have to follow from the null-boundary action and its state space.

There is also a finite-dimensional obstruction to importing the polynomial Weyl algebra unchanged into a projected size sector. For finite square matrices X,Y , cyclicity gives Tr ⁡ ([X,Y ]) = 0. If [X,Y ] = 𝑖ℏ1 on a nonzero finite-dimensional space, the right side has trace 𝑖ℏ times its dimension, a contradiction. Projecting a canonical pair defined on a larger space need not preserve its commutator. With a projection P and bounded operators X,Y one has exactly

[𝑃𝑋𝑃,𝑃𝑌 P] = P[X,Y ]P − 𝑃𝑋(1 − P)Y P + 𝑃𝑌 (1 − P)𝑋𝑃.

Even for bounded inputs the two cross terms can carry the difference. For unbounded canonical generators further common-domain conditions are needed before writing the same expression. Chandrasekaran’s program distinguishes a representation at the level of boundary observables from bounds under a restriction on the size of a diamond. A purported comparison has to identify the restriction and recompute what survives it. Declaring an algebraic Weyl relation exact on a finite block would fail the trace test.

14.4 Local smearing and the total area

For a smooth regular cut, the integrated generator in Chandrasekaran’s equation (3.43) sums a charge aspect. Its value records the area of the cut but loses its angular distribution. In §4.2 the incoming data at fixed total area can fluctuate in the distribution of area over the corner. Thus comparing a uniform boost on the whole cut and comparing separately labelled probes on corner patches are physically different requests [1].

A two-cell measurement makes the difference explicit with no assumption of full field quantization. Suppose two labelled commuting positive observables A1,A2 take values l and h, with 0 < l < h. On states diagonal in the joint spectral basis the data are ordinary probability distributions on four pairs. Consider preparations

ν+ = 1 2δ(l,l) + 1 2δ(h,h),ν− = 1 2δ(l,h) + 1 2δ(h,l).

Each individual cell has the same probability for both outcomes in both preparations. All single-cell boost characteristic functions consequently agree at all rapidities. Yet the total area is either 2l or 2h in the first preparation and is always l + h in the second. If t is the calibrated coefficient of A1 + A2 in the phase, the two expectations are

e𝑖𝑡(l+h) cos⁡(t(h − l))𝑎𝑛𝑑e𝑖𝑡(l+h).

Taking t = π∕(2(h − l)) gives zero for the first expectation and unit modulus for the second. The discrepancy is a cross correlation, and does not come from either one-cell spectral measure.

There is also a converse failure. Sharp preparations at (l,h) and (h,l) give identical total area and identical expectation for every uniform boost. An operation sensitive to the first labelled cell resolves them. Equality of total boost data would be sufficient in a model in which only the total area is an observable. Once a proposed class of decorated embeddings includes a local patch smearing, the comparison needs additional state data.

In the two-cell algebra all joint information is accessible from

χ(t1,t2) = ∑ x,y∈{l,h}ν(x,y)ei(t1x+t2y).

For instance choose t∗ = π∕(h − l). The function eit∗(x−l) is + 1 at l and − 1 at h. The three expectations of the sign on the first cell, the sign on the second cell, and their product determine the four probabilities along with normalization. This gives a finite set of tests on the joint state in this particular spectrum. A physical corner would need independently specified smearings, operators and a family of preparations before the same reasoning could be applied.

A change of angular coordinates on a cut provides a kinematical consistency equation for such a choice. Let a classical patch measurement on a surface S be AS[f] = ∫ SfdμS, with f a fixed scalar test function and dμS the area density. For an orientation-preserving identification ξ : S → S′ suppose ξ∗dμS′ = dμS. Then

AS′[f ∘ ξ−1] = A S[f].

This follows from change of variables and is an equation on labelled measurements. A mere assertion that two totals agree is its special case f = 1. In a larger diamond the old corner can cease to be part of the boundary, and a map ξ of the required kind need not exist. Extending the measurement into the larger gravitational algebra would then require an explicit dressing or an equivalent protocol for recovering the patch. Assuming a map of angular coordinates fixes that problem only for cuts already identified.

The finite example also clarifies a requirement on any entropy comparison. Let Md(ℂ) act on ℂd ⊗ ℂm as a↦a ⊗ 1m. For any density matrices ρ,σ on the two factors, the state ρ ⊗ σ has expectation Tr ⁡ (𝜌𝑎) on every observable in the image. If σ is the maximally mixed state, the eigenvalues of ρ ⊗ σ are the eigenvalues of ρ divided by m, each repeated m times. Its von Neumann entropy therefore exceeds that of ρ by log⁡m. The representation has changed while all expectations on the smaller algebra agree. A full entropy claim must consequently identify which degrees of freedom and which trace enter the state count. In particular equality of area distributions under all probes on a single cut is insufficient to identify a gravitational entropy across different choices of observable algebra.

15 Tests on null data

The transverse geometry of a null sheet differs from the model of translations on T∗V . Chandrasekaran’s equations (3.39) and (3.42) exhibit shear and corner terms in the symplectic potential and its exterior derivative. The area-dependent boost charge couples to variations of the conformal metric and to changes of the null normals. In particular it would be unjustified to infer a direct tensor factor for the corner charge from the independent pairs in the present example. His Appendix D.1 also distinguishes first-class and second-class constraints in its reduced measure. Equating every constraint with the generator of a group action would lose this distinction.

For comparison, on one canonical plane the constraints s = 0 and n = 0 have {s,n} = 1. Their zero set is a point, and the matrix of mutual brackets is invertible. Reducing this second-class pair by the Dirac bracket removes the plane [9]. Tacking on a spectator pair (r,k) leaves its bracket unchanged because the spectator functions commute with both constraints. Treating s,n as commuting generators of gauge translations on the common zero locus would instead require a translation tangent to that locus. Neither translation is tangent there. This finite example shows how the rank of the constraint bracket, a separate piece of input, changes the meaning of a quotient.

A physical calculation can begin on a regular family of decorated diamonds. Choose a cut without a generator crossing before the specified corner, choose two frame patches and specify the observable carried by a common smearing at their interface. Fix which perturbations obey the null constraints and boundary conditions. For these perturbations the kernel of the pulled-back symplectic form determines the available degeneracies. A transformation with a nonzero Hamiltonian surface charge is separately recorded whenever the boundary condition permits it. At this step the algebraic comparison needs an actual map on the reduced variables and on changes of frame.

At the quantum step one can ask whether a proposed transport preserves the chosen charge sector, changes to another specified sector, or has an anomaly on a common domain. Equations (3) and (4) are one formulation for normalizer presentations. If one quantizes the reduced phase space directly, the analogous question is whether the map respects its reduced brackets and quantum relations and can be implemented in the chosen state class. A proof for boost observables alone would establish a comparison on that subalgebra. The radiative fields and the couplings of the corner would remain to be addressed.

A separate question concerns the source of the categorical observable. In the cited finite reconstruction, relational weights give a metric on a graph [4]. To turn a collection of probes into a copy of the corner algebra requires an independent prescription for extracting the measured coordinates, relations, involution and gauge identifications. Reading them from the null-cone algebra and declaring those objects to be the probes would make the comparison tautological. An informative first observable would have a stated measurement on each side and a test of equality after transporting it through one nonidentity arrow.

16 Conclusion

A common frame constraint leaves a relative reading and its conjugate charge measurable. This conclusion holds in the degree-zero Poisson algebra and in the quantized normalizer quotient of the translation model. The dependent tests used to describe the constraint persist in negative Koszul degree. On a network, a quantum character inconsistent around a cycle kills the full quotient even when it passes every test on a tree. These calculations put a concrete condition on any proposed gravitational realization. It must carry the physical generators and their quantum constraint levels consistently across changes of boundary frame.

The rank-changing family identifies an operator that disappears if one reduces before specializing. At fixed rank the state comparison still detects inequivalent preparations through their displacement widths, and two implementers can leave a phase around a square that every conjugation on observables misses. At a null cut, one physical test is available already. Two independent descriptions can compare the spectral distribution of the area-generated boost, the normalized rapidity pairing, and the joint measurements carried by identified corner patches. Agreement there would give a correspondence for specified boundary data while leaving the radiative algebra to be determined.
